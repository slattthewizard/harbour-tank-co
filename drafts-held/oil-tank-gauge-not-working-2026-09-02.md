# Oil Tank Gauge Not Working? How to Check Your Real Fuel Level

Your gauge has said half a tank since Christmas. Is it really half, or did the float quit weeks ago while the oil kept burning?

That's the trouble with an oil tank gauge not working. It doesn't flash a warning or make a noise. It just sits there looking normal until the furnace goes quiet at five in the morning in the middle of a February blow. Around St. John's, that means a cold house, a scramble for an emergency delivery, and sometimes a furnace tech to get things running again.

The good news is that you don't need the gauge to know how much oil you've got. A clean stick and five minutes will tell you the truth. This guide covers why float gauges fail, how to check your real fuel level in litres, and the point where a stuck gauge becomes a tank problem.

## Why an Oil Tank Gauge Stops Working

Almost every residential tank on the Avalon uses the same simple float gauge. A hinged arm hangs inside the tank with a float on the end. As the oil goes down, the float drops, the arm swings, and a plastic disc slides down inside the clear dome on top of the tank. No power, no wires, no calibration. It's about as basic as a gauge gets.

That simplicity is also why it fails quietly. The common causes are:

- **A stuck float arm.** Sludge builds up on the tank bottom over the years. When the float dips into it, the arm can bind and stay put while the oil level keeps dropping.
- **A waterlogged float.** Older cork floats soak up oil and sink. Plastic floats can crack. Either way, the disc drops to empty even with plenty of oil in the tank.
- **A fogged or cracked dome.** Rain and melting snow work into the dome through a loose cap or a hairline crack. You end up staring at condensation instead of a reading.
- **A bent arm.** If the tank was moved, jarred by a snow blower, or knocked during a delivery, the arm can catch on the tank wall or on internal rust scale.

The signs are easy to spot. The reading hasn't moved in weeks. The reading jumps when you tap the dome. The disc sits at full after a delivery of only a few hundred litres. Any one of those means the number on top of your tank is a guess, not a measurement.

One caution before you touch anything. If you can see rust weeping through the paint, a wet patch under the tank, or a HOST tag with a date that's already passed, the gauge is the least of your worries. ## Three Quick Checks Before You Grab a Stick

You can rule a lot in or out without any tools.

**Tap the dome.** A light tap with your knuckle is sometimes enough to free a float that's hung up on a bit of sludge. If the disc drops an inch or two, you've found your problem.

**Check your delivery slip.** A standard 200-gallon tank holds about 910 litres. If the driver put in 600 litres and the gauge went from a quarter to full, the math works. If the driver put in 200 litres and the gauge went from empty to full, the gauge is lying.

**Do the burn math.** In a St. John's January, a typical oil-heated home burns somewhere around 8 to 15 litres a day, more during a cold snap or if you've got the thermostat cranked. Count the days since your last delivery, multiply, and subtract. If the gauge says you've burned nothing in three weeks, you know better.

Picture Gerald in Mount Pearl. His gauge sat at half from mid-December right through January, so he figured he was fine. On a Sunday morning in early February the furnace went cold, and the stick showed a fingertip of oil in the bottom of the tank. The emergency delivery charge and a furnace tech to bleed the line ran him about $350 before he'd bought a single litre of oil. A stick check every two weeks would've cost him nothing.

## How to Check Your Real Fuel Level With a Dipstick

This is the one method that works on every tank, indoor or outdoor, steel or fibreglass.

**What you need:** a clean, straight, dry stick that's at least 48 inches long. A length of one-by-two, a wooden dowel, or a proper tank stick from the hardware store all work. Wood is better than metal because the oil line shows clearly on it. Don't use anything short enough to slip out of your hand and into the tank.

**Step 1: Find the fill pipe.** On an outdoor tank it's the two-inch pipe with a screw cap, usually at one end near the top. On an indoor tank the fill pipe runs through the wall to the outside, so you'll be doing this from outdoors. Don't unscrew the gauge dome itself to get a stick in. The threads are fragile and you can drop the float into the tank.

**Step 2: Open the cap and lower the stick straight down.** Go slowly until it touches the bottom. Hold it still for a second so the oil line sets, then lift it straight out.

**Step 3: Measure the wet part.** Measure from the bottom of the stick to the top of the oil line, in inches. That's your depth.

**Step 4: Convert depth to litres.** Most Avalon homes have a standard 200-gallon oval tank that's about 44 inches tall. Use the chart below as a rough guide. The tank is rounded at the top and bottom, so an inch near the ends holds less oil than an inch in the middle.

| Depth on the stick | Approximate oil in a standard 910 L (200-gallon) tank | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| 6 inches | About 90 L | Order today. You're in the sludge zone. |
| 10 inches | About 170 L | Order this week. |
| 14 inches | About 260 L | Roughly a quarter. Book a delivery. |
| 22 inches | About 450 L | Half a tank. |
| 30 inches | About 630 L | Comfortable. |
| 38 inches | About 810 L | Nearly full. |
| 42 inches | About 880 L | Full. Don't top up. |

If your tank has its own chart from the manufacturer, use that instead. A 100-gallon backup tank, a horizontal tank, or a Roth double-wall tank all have different shapes, and the numbers above won't apply.

**Step 5: Wipe the stick, close the cap, and write it down.** A tight cap matters. An open or loose fill cap lets snow and rain straight into the tank, and water in the oil is a bigger headache than a dead gauge. Put the date and the depth on a scrap of paper on the fridge.

A few safety points. No open flames, no smoking, no leaning on an outdoor tank. If the fill cap is seized, don't force it. If the tank stand is leaning, the legs are rusted through, or the tank is sitting in a puddle of its own oil, stop and call a professional. Those aren't stick problems.

## When an Oil Tank Gauge That Isn't Working Points to a Bigger Problem

Sometimes the gauge is the only thing wrong. Sometimes it's the first thing you notice on a tank with other issues. Here's how to tell the difference while you've got the cap off.

**Look at the stick.** If the bottom inch is black and gritty, that's sludge, and a thick layer of it is what jams float arms in the first place. A clear or milky layer under the oil is water. Both mean the tank needs attention beyond a new gauge. We cover the water problem in detail in [water in your oil tank](/blog/water-in-oil-tank/).

**Look at the tank.** Rust streaks, bubbling paint, a damp patch at a seam, or a wet spot on the ground underneath. Any of those and the gauge is a side issue.

**Look at the tag.** Every registered tank in Newfoundland and Labrador carries a metal HOST tag with a six-digit number and an expiry stamped in month-year form. A tag reading 7-2028 means the tank can't be used after the end of July 2028. Under the province's [Heating Oil Storage Tank System Regulations](https://www.assembly.nl.ca/legislation/sr/regulations/rc030060.htm), a delivery driver isn't allowed to fill a tank without a valid, unexpired label. If you're not sure how to read yours, our guide on [how to read a HOST tag](/blog/how-to-read-host-tag/) walks through it.

Take Dave out in Torbay. His gauge had been dead since the fall, so he went out with a stick one Saturday to check the level before a cold snap. While he had the cap off he noticed the tag: 3-2026. It had expired months earlier and nobody had caught it. His oil company's next driver did catch it, and the truck left without pumping. Dave ended up with a new 200-gallon painted steel tank installed for $3,200, old tank removed and disposed of, and a fresh tag good for years. Fixing the gauge on the old tank would've been money down the drain.

A gauge that quits on a tank that's ten or fifteen years old is often a tank near the end of its life. Outdoor single-wall steel tanks get 10 to 20 years under the regulations depending on the gauge of steel and where the outlet is. If your tag runs out within the next year, put the gauge money toward the [replacement tank](/oil-tank-replacement-st-johns/) instead.

## Keeping Track of Your Oil Through a St. John's Winter

A working gauge is nice. A habit is better. Here's what keeps Avalon homes from running dry.

**Stick the tank every two weeks from November to April.** Weekly in a cold snap. It's the only reading you can trust.

**Never let it drop under a quarter.** Below that, the pickup line starts pulling from the sludge layer, condensation forms on the bare tank walls, and you're one cold night from air in the line. If you do run dry, our post on [running out of heating oil](/blog/ran-out-of-heating-oil/) covers how to get the furnace going again and when to call for help.

**Keep the fill pipe and vent clear.** A snowbank over the fill means the driver can't deliver even if you've ordered.

**Ask about automatic delivery.** Most suppliers can put you on a schedule based on degree days. It doesn't replace the stick, but it catches the weeks you forget.

**Check the tag every fall.** Expiry dates sneak up. A tank that expires in March can't be filled in March, and that's the worst month on the Avalon to find out. If the date is close, [an expired tag is an emergency we handle](/expired-oil-tank-emergency-st-johns/), but it's cheaper and calmer to plan the swap for September. The province's [Petroleum Storage and Management section](https://www.gov.nl.ca/eccc/env-protection/petroleum/) publishes the owner brochures if you want the official wording on tags and registration.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I replace an oil tank float gauge myself?

On a standard outdoor tank, yes, many homeowners do. The gauge unscrews from the top port, and a new float gauge threads into the same hole. Make sure the float arm is the right length for your tank height and the seal is snug so rain stays out. If the old gauge is seized or you can see rust around the port, have your furnace tech do it during the annual service.

### Why does my oil tank gauge read full when the tank is nearly empty?

The float arm is stuck at the top, usually on rust scale on the inside wall or because the float has jammed against the tank top after a full delivery. A gentle tap on the dome sometimes frees it. If it doesn't, check the level with a stick right away, because a gauge stuck at full is the one that leaves people without heat.

### How accurate is a float gauge when it is working?

Not very. Even a healthy float gauge is a rough indicator, and it tends to drop fast in the last quarter because of the tank's rounded bottom. Treat quarter marks as approximate, and use a stick when you need a real number, especially before ordering a delivery or heading into a cold week.

### Will the delivery driver refuse to fill my tank if the gauge is broken?

A broken gauge on its own won't stop a delivery. What stops a delivery is an expired or missing HOST tag, a visible leak, or a tank the driver considers unsafe. Under provincial rules the driver has to report those problems to you and to the province, so a refused delivery usually means paperwork as well as a cold house. Check the tag before the truck arrives, not after.
