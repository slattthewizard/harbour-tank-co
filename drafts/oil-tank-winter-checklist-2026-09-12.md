# Your Oil Tank Winter Checklist Before the First St. John's Cold Snap

The dipstick comes up wetter and shorter than you expected, and it's only the third week of September. You wipe it down, stick it again, and start doing the math on how many weeks that buys you before the first real cold snap.

That five minutes with a stick is item one on any oil tank winter checklist worth running, and most of what's left takes less time than clearing the driveway after the first snowfall. September and October are the weeks to do this. By January every installer on the Avalon has a full book, and the first northeaster has already found whatever you missed in the fall.

This isn't a professional inspection. It's a walk-around you can do yourself this weekend, in about half an hour, with a flashlight and a rag. It won't replace the licensed inspector who tags and registers a new system, but it will tell you whether you need to call one before the snow flies or whether you're set for another season.

## Read the tag before you touch anything else

Start at the tag, not the tank. Every registered heating oil system in Newfoundland and Labrador carries a metal HOST tag with a six-digit number and an expiry stamped as month and year, something like 07-2028. That date is the one number on the whole tank that actually matters, because a delivery driver who reads an expired tag has to leave without filling.

Picture a homeowner in Kelligrews doing exactly this walk-around on a Saturday in September. The tag on her outdoor tank reads 11-2026, two months away. She isn't due for a delivery yet, but she knows the fall rush will book up every installer on the Avalon within weeks, and she'd rather arrange a swap now than find out in November that nobody has a slot before the tank turns illegal to fill. She calls [(709) 800-3117](tel:+17098003117) that afternoon and has a written estimate in hand by Monday.

If your tag reads next spring or later, you're fine for this winter and can move to the next item. If it reads this winter, or you can't find a tag at all, that's the one thing on this list that changes the whole plan. An [oil tank replacement](/oil-tank-replacement-st-johns/) booked in September is a routine same-week job. The same swap booked after the tank has already gone illegal, or after a driver has already refused a fill, moves into a different category entirely, and we'll get to that at the end of this list. The province's [petroleum storage page](https://www.gov.nl.ca/eccc/env-protection/petroleum/) has the background on the HOST program if you'd rather read the source than take our word for it.

## Stick the tank instead of trusting the gauge alone

A working gauge is a nice thing to have and not something to rely on by itself. Gauges stick, especially after a summer of sitting mostly full, and a stuck float can read half a tank when the tank is nearly dry. The only sure way to know is the same thing you did before you started reading this: pull the stick, wipe it, drop it back in, and read the wet mark.

For scale, a 200-gallon tank holds about 900 litres, and a 100-gallon tank holds about 450 litres. Knowing roughly how many litres are left, against how many weeks of cold weather are coming, tells you whether you need to book a delivery this month or whether you have some room to wait.

If the gauge and the stick don't agree, or if the gauge hasn't moved in weeks no matter how much oil goes in or out, don't just live with it. [This guide to a stuck oil tank gauge](/blog/oil-tank-gauge-not-working/) covers the dipstick method in more detail and what usually causes a gauge to fail. And if you've already run the tank dry once this year, [restarting a furnace after running out of heating oil](/blog/ran-out-of-heating-oil/) is worth reading before it happens again in January, when getting a technician out is a much longer wait.

## Clear the vent, the whistle and the fill cap

Three small parts do a surprising amount of work. The vent lets air out of the tank as oil goes in. The whistle, fitted to the vent on most residential systems, is supposed to sound while the tank is filling and go quiet once it's full, which is how the driver knows when to stop. The fill cap keeps rain, snowmelt and anything else out of the pipe between deliveries.

Check that the vent isn't blocked by a wasp's nest, a build-up of dead leaves, or a coat of paint from the last time someone touched up the shed. Check the whistle is still attached and hasn't rusted off its fitting. Check the fill cap seats properly and isn't cracked, because a loose or missing cap is one of the easier ways for water to get into a tank over a wet Avalon fall.

None of this takes more than a couple of minutes, and none of it needs a tool beyond your hand and a flashlight. It's also one of the few items on this checklist a driver will notice on delivery day, since a blocked vent or a missing whistle can hold up a fill on the spot.

## Check the legs, the pad and the fuel line

Take a look at what the tank is standing on. Steel legs on a concrete or block pad should sit level and solid, with no visible sinking into the ground beside the pad. A tank that leans, even slightly, pools water and sludge at the low end instead of near the outlet, which is exactly the condition that shortens a tank's working life from the inside.

Take a typical 1980s bungalow in Mount Pearl, the kind with an outdoor tank on the same wooden-edged pad it's had for decades. Years of frost heave had tipped the pad just enough that the tank leaned toward the fence line. The owner caught it on a fall walk-around, before it turned into a real problem, and had the stand levelled and reset when the [oil tank service crew in Mount Pearl](/oil-tank-service-mount-pearl/) was out for an unrelated call. A lean like that's cheap to fix early and a lot less cheap to ignore.

If your own tank looks like it's doing something similar, a quick photo is often enough for us to give you an opinion on fix versus replace, and getting a free written estimate costs you nothing.

While you're down there, follow the copper fuel line from the tank to the wall. It should be a smooth, continuous run with no kinks, no green or white corrosion on the fittings, and no oily sheen on the ground underneath. Rust on the tank itself is its own subject. [This guide to rust on an oil tank](/blog/rust-on-oil-tank/) explains which kind is cosmetic and which kind means the tank is on borrowed time, and it's worth five minutes if you spotted anything orange while you were down there.

## Think about what a St. John's winter will do to the tank

A tank that looks fine in September can end up in trouble by February, and it's usually the roof's fault, not the tank's. A lot of Avalon houses shed heavy snow straight off a lower roofline onto whatever sits below, and if that happens to be the fill pipe, the vent or the tank itself, a big dump after a northeaster can bury all three under wet snow overnight.

Take a retired teacher in Torbay who found this out the hard way a couple of winters back. A roof shed dropped enough snow onto the tank's vent pipe that a delivery had to be rescheduled because the driver couldn't find it, let alone reach it. Now the first thing on the fall list is checking where the snow will land, and clearing a path after every storm rather than after the oil runs low.

Wind is the other one. Loose ground, a stand that was never quite level, and a hard gust off the harbour have been enough to shift a tank on its legs in a bad enough blow. If your tank sits in an exposed spot, that's one more reason the legs-and-pad check above is worth doing before the weather turns, not after.

## Get the furnace and filter serviced before the first cold snap

A tank in good shape doesn't help much if the furnace can't use what's in it. A fuel filter that hasn't been changed since spring is a common reason a furnace that was running fine in September starts cutting out in November, right when you need it most. Have your furnace technician change the filter and give the burner a look before the heating season starts in earnest, not after the first cold night finds the problem for you.

If the furnace does stop taking oil even with a full tank, the usual suspects run from a clogged filter to an air lock to a frozen line, and [this walkthrough of a furnace not getting oil](/blog/furnace-not-getting-oil/) covers the checks worth doing before you call anyone out. Water sitting in the bottom of the tank is another common cause, and if your dipstick came up wet in the first step of this checklist, [this guide to water in an oil tank](/blog/water-in-oil-tank/) explains where it comes from and what to do about it.

## Finish the checklist by booking your delivery early

Here's the whole walk-around in one table, roughly in the order it's worth doing it.

| Checklist item | What good looks like | Call someone if |
|---|---|---|
| HOST tag expiry | Stamped date is next spring or later | The date falls this winter, or you can't find a tag |
| Oil level, by dipstick | Stick shows enough for the weeks of cold ahead | Gauge and stick disagree, or the gauge hasn't moved in weeks |
| Vent | Clear of leaves, nests and paint | Air can't be heard moving, or it looks blocked |
| Whistle | Attached, and sounds during a fill | It's missing, rusted off or silent while filling |
| Fill cap | Seats tightly, no cracks | Cap is loose, cracked or won't seat |
| Legs and pad | Level, no sinking at the base | Tank leans, or the ground beside the pad has sunk |
| Fuel line | Smooth run, no kinks or corrosion, no oily smell | Line shows green or white corrosion, or a kink |
| Path to the tank | Clear enough for a driver with a hose in the dark | Snow, a shed roof or debris will block it this winter |

Once you've been through the table, book your first delivery of the season before the fall rush fills every supplier's schedule at once. If everything checked out, that's the whole job done for another year. If the tag is the item that failed, deal with that one first, because everything else on this list is a maintenance question and an expired tag is a legal one.

A tank that's already past its date, or a driver who's already refused a fill, isn't a fall project anymore. That's an [expired tank emergency](/expired-oil-tank-emergency-st-johns/), and it moves to the front of the line. For everything short of that, send a photo of your tag through the [contact form](/#contact) and ask for a free written estimate, so you know the number before you need it, not after.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should I keep the tank full over winter?
Keeping it above a quarter tank is a reasonable habit, since a fuller tank leaves less air space for condensation to form on the cold steel above the oil line. It also gives you more of a buffer if a cold snap or a delivery delay stretches out longer than planned. It isn't a hard rule, just good practice for an Avalon winter.

### Can snow crush an oil tank?
Snow alone won't crush a properly supported steel or fibreglass tank, but a heavy roof shed landing directly on the vent or fill pipe can block both, and enough weight against a tank that's already leaning can make a bad lean worse. The bigger snow risk is burying the path a driver needs to reach the tank, not the tank itself.

### How early should I book the first oil delivery?
Book it in September, or as soon as your fall walk-around is done, rather than waiting until the tank runs low. The fall rush fills up every installer's and supplier's schedule fast, and a tank that also needs a tag or a repair takes longer to sort out once everyone else is booked solid too.

### What if the tag expires in February?
A tag with only a few months left doesn't mean an emergency in September, but it does mean this is the season to plan the swap rather than wait for the tank to expire mid-winter. Booking a same-week replacement now, while installers still have room on the schedule, is a lot easier than doing it after a driver has already refused a fill in the cold.
