# Oil Furnace Filter Change: How Often Should You Do It?

The furnace coughs, runs for a minute and then goes quiet, and the tank outside is still half full. When that happens in a cold snap, the fuel oil filter is one of the first things a service tech looks at, and it's a small, cheap part that causes a lot of grief. An oil furnace filter change is about as routine as home heating maintenance gets, yet plenty of homeowners never look at theirs.

This is the fuel oil filter on the line between your tank and your burner. It isn't the air filter that sits in the return duct. Mix the two up and you can honestly say "I changed the filter" while the furnace still won't stay lit.

## What the fuel oil filter does on your line

Oil leaves the tank through a pipe at the end, top or bottom of the shell. It travels along a small copper or flexible line to the furnace, and somewhere along that run there's a filter. It might be a canister with a replaceable cartridge, a small glass or metal housing, or a spin-on unit that looks a bit like a car's oil filter.

Its job is to catch what you don't want in the burner: rust flakes, tank-floor sludge, bits of scale, a bit of water. The nozzle inside your furnace has an opening finer than a pencil lead. Anything that gets past the filter can block that opening, and then the furnace locks out.

Think of the filter as the cheap part that protects the expensive part. It's meant to clog before the nozzle does.

## How often to change the oil filter on your furnace

There's no single number that fits every house. The usual practice, and the one most service techs follow, is to change the filter at the annual furnace service. That visit is the natural time because the tech is already opening up the burner, checking the nozzle and cleaning the unit.

Beyond the yearly service, a handful of situations call for a fresh filter sooner:

- After a tank replacement, because a new tank, new line fittings and fresh oil all stir things up.
- After the tank has run dry, since the last of the oil carries whatever has settled on the floor.
- If sludge or water has shown up anywhere in the system.
- If the furnace has locked out more than once with oil in the tank.

Those situations are covered in the table below, and the first two rows are the ones most people forget. A filter that's two seasons old and sitting between a freshly swapped tank and a burner is asking for trouble.

| Situation | Change the filter? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Annual furnace service | Yes, as a matter of routine | The tech already has the system open, and a fresh cartridge starts the season clean |
| Tank has just been replaced | Yes | New fittings and a fresh fill can shake loose debris from the line |
| Tank ran completely dry | Yes, before restarting if you can | The last oil in a tank is the dirtiest, and it all heads for the filter |
| Water or sludge found in the tank | Yes, and sooner than later | Water and residue load the filter fast; the source needs dealing with too |
| Furnace lock-outs with oil in the tank | Check it first | A clogged filter is a common cause of a starved burner |
| Furnace running fine, filter changed last year | Not yet | Leave a working system alone until the next service |
| Heat pump home that burns little oil | Still on the annual schedule | Oil sitting for months collects sediment even if the burner rarely runs |

That last row matters on the Avalon, where many homes added ductless heat pumps and kept the oil furnace for the coldest weeks. A furnace that runs a fraction as often doesn't mean a cleaner filter. Oil that sits for a long stretch can still drop sediment, and the first hard cold night of the season is when it all gets pulled to the filter at once.

## Signs the filter is clogged

A clogged filter doesn't announce itself. It shows up as a handful of symptoms that look like other problems, which is why [the seven checks between tank and burner](/blog/furnace-not-getting-oil/) are worth running through in order when the heat stops.

Here's what points at the filter specifically:

- The furnace starts, runs briefly, then shuts off and needs the reset button.
- Heat output seems weak, or the flame looks uneven when you can see it through the sight glass.
- You see dark sediment or cloudiness in a glass-bowl filter.
- A cartridge that's been in place for a couple of years and has never been touched.
- Lock-outs that cluster on the coldest nights, when the oil is thicker and moves more slowly through a loaded filter.

None of these proves the filter is the culprit. They do make it the cheapest and most sensible place to start. A filter change that fixes the problem has told you something useful about what's coming from the tank.

## When to leave it to the service tech

Changing a fuel oil filter is not complicated in principle. In practice there are two things that catch homeowners out.

First, the line has to be bled afterward. Air gets into the line when the filter is opened, and a furnace with air in the line will lock out until the air is purged. Bleeding means opening a small valve at the burner pump with a container underneath, and doing it wrong can sprinkle oil across the floor.

Second, oil spills. A filter housing holds a small amount of oil, and the line behind it can drain when you open it. On a cold basement floor or a wet outdoor pad, that's a cleanup you'd rather not have. The regulations also expect reasonable precautions to prevent and contain spills when a system is worked on, and a tech arrives with the rags, the catch pan and the habit of using them.

Honestly, if you've never done it, the first time shouldn't be a February night with the house cooling down. Book the tech. If you do want to learn, ask to watch the next annual service and see how it's done on your particular unit.

If the furnace has already stopped because the tank ran dry, there's a separate sequence for getting it going again, and [restarting after running out of oil](/blog/ran-out-of-heating-oil/) walks through it.

## Why a filter that keeps clogging points at the tank

A filter that clogs every few months isn't a filter problem. It's telling you something about what's inside the tank.

Steel tanks rust from the inside, and they do it at the bottom. When the tank sits partly empty, condensation puts water on the floor of the shell, and that water starts the corrosion in the one spot you can't see. Rust flakes and sludge accumulate down there, and every delivery and every cold snap stirs a bit more of it toward the outlet. How water gets in and how to get it out is the subject of [the guide to water in an oil tank](/blog/water-in-oil-tank/), and it's worth reading if your filter is dirtier than it should be.

Picture a 1980s bungalow in Mount Pearl with an outdoor 200-gallon steel tank, stamped 6-2027 on its HOST tag. The filter has been changed twice in one winter and both cartridges came out dark and gritty. Changing a third filter isn't the answer there. The tank is near the end of its legal life, the bottom is likely shedding rust, and the sensible plan is a swap before the tag runs out, with a clean new tank and a new filter at the same time.

A new filter is a repair. A new tank is the fix when the source of the debris is the steel itself.

If the outside of your tank shows rust, that's a different question. [Which rust is cosmetic and which means replace](/blog/rust-on-oil-tank/) is covered separately, though the inside is what feeds your filter.

## Oil furnace filter change, the regulations and your tag

In the regulations, the "system" isn't just the tank. Under NLR 60/03 it's the tank plus the vent, fill and withdrawal piping and filters, right up to where the line connects to the furnace. So your filter is legally part of the registered system.

The word "alter" matters here too. To alter a system means to add, remove, replace, repair or reconfigure any part of it, and that includes the filter. Swapping the cartridge at the annual service is routine maintenance that service techs do every season. If you're changing the filter housing, moving the filter to a different position on the line or rerouting piping, ask the installer's licensed inspector or your service company how it's classed before the work starts.

The regulations also say heating oil can't be delivered to a system without a valid, unexpired registration label. That's the HOST tag on the tank. A fresh filter does nothing for an expired tag, and a driver who reads a lapsed date leaves without filling. If you're not sure what your tag says, [how to read the HOST tag](/blog/how-to-read-host-tag/) takes about two minutes. The department's own petroleum page explains the program: [the province's petroleum storage page](https://www.gov.nl.ca/eccc/env-protection/petroleum/).

## Frequently asked questions

### How often should I change the oil filter on my furnace?
Most homes do it once a year, at the annual furnace service. Change it sooner after a tank swap, after running out of oil, or if sludge or water has turned up. If the filter looks clean and the furnace runs fine, the annual schedule is enough.

### Can I change the fuel oil filter myself?
It's possible, but the line needs bleeding afterward and the housing can drain oil onto the floor. If you haven't done it before, have a service tech do it and watch how it's done. A spill indoors in winter is a bad afternoon.

### Does changing the filter fix a furnace that keeps locking out?
Sometimes. A clogged filter is a common cause, so it's a sensible first step. If the new filter clogs again within weeks, the debris is coming from the tank, and the fix is dealing with the water or sludge, or replacing the tank.

### Is the oil filter part of my registered tank system?
Yes. The regulations define the system as the tank plus the vent, fill and withdrawal piping and filters, up to where the line meets the furnace. A cartridge swap at the annual service is routine maintenance. Moving or replacing the housing or the piping is an alteration under s.2(d), so ask your installer or service company first.
