# Heating Oil Gelling in the Cold: Why Outdoor Tanks Struggle and What Helps

It's the coldest night of a northeaster, the furnace has locked out, and the stick says you've still got half a tank. You check the breaker, you check the thermostat, you press the reset button and it quits again. Heating oil gelling is one of the few ways to end up with no heat while the tank still has oil in it.

This isn't a rare freak event, and it isn't always a sign your tank is failing. It's what cold does to oil that has to travel through a thin line and a small filter. Below we'll cover why outdoor tanks get hit hardest, how to tell gelling from other problems, and what to ask your supplier. ## What heating oil gelling is

Furnace oil carries a bit of wax. In hard cold, that wax starts to thicken the oil and cloud it, and in the worst case it builds up into a slush that won't pour. Oil that thick doesn't move well through a small copper line, and the wax collects in the filter first because the filter is the narrowest spot on the run.

The regulations recognise two grades. Heating oil under NLR 60/03 includes Type 1, commonly called stove oil, and Type 2, commonly called furnace oil (s.2(j)). Stove oil is the thinner of the two, and furnace oil is what most Avalon oil furnaces burn.

We're not going to give you a temperature at which it happens. It depends on the oil, how long it's been in the tank and how exposed everything is. What we can say is that gelling shows up in the worst cold, usually after a few days of it, and it tends to hit the setups with the most exposed hardware.

## Why outdoor tanks struggle on the Avalon

An outdoor steel tank sits in the wind with nothing between the oil and the air. The tank walls take on the outside temperature, the line runs along cold siding or across open ground, and the filter often hangs right there in the draught. Add a northeaster pushing wind-driven snow and freezing rain against all of it, and the oil in the thinnest parts of the system is the first to thicken.

A half-empty tank doesn't help either. There's more steel exposed to the cold above the oil line, and more condensation forming inside. Water in the tank is a separate problem, and our [water in oil tank guide](/blog/water-in-oil-tank/) covers it, but frozen water and waxed oil can look alike from the furnace's side. That's part of why people mix them up.

Snow matters in its own way. A roof shed can bury the fill pipe, the vent and the line under a drift, and then the first thaw refreezes it into ice. We'd put "go look at the tank in daylight, in a coat" ahead of most of the troubleshooting you'll find online.

## Heating oil gelling or something else? How to tell

Several problems make a furnace lock out, and gelling is only one. The table sorts the usual symptoms. Our [furnace not getting oil guide](/blog/furnace-not-getting-oil/) walks through the general no-oil diagnosis, so use this table as a first pass only.

| Symptom | Likely cause | What to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Furnace locks out on the coldest night, tank has oil | Waxed oil in the filter or line | Call your service tech; ask your supplier about winter blends |
| Filter bowl looks cloudy, milky or thick | Wax, or water mixed in | Leave the filter to a tech, since opening it can spill oil |
| Furnace runs fine in mild weather, fails in hard cold | Gelling in an exposed line or filter | Shield the line and ask about winter oil |
| Gauge reads fine but no oil reaches the furnace | Blocked line, filter or fuel pump | Have a tech check the whole run |
| Sputtering, then quitting, after a delivery | Air in the line or stirred-up sludge | Service call; a fresh filter often fixes it |
| Tank reads empty | You've run dry | See our [ran out of heating oil guide](/blog/ran-out-of-heating-oil/) |

If a filter is cloudy and you can't tell wax from water, don't guess. A service tech can tell in a minute, and they carry what's needed to catch the oil.

## What helps when it's cold

The honest list is short, and most of it is boring. First, ask your supplier about winter blends or additives. We're not naming products or promising what any company sells, because that's between you and them. A call in the fall, before the cold, is easier than one at midnight in January.

Second, don't pour anything into the tank yourself. A bottle of something off a shelf, tipped into a tank you can't see inside, is a gamble with a furnace that costs far more than the bottle. Ask the supplier first and let them tell you what suits your oil.

Third, shield the exposed parts. An uninsulated line running along a north wall, or across open ground, is the usual weak point. Wrapping or boxing the line and keeping it out of the wind helps, as does keeping the filter somewhere sheltered. Any change to the filter or the piping is an alteration to the system (s.2(d)), so check with your contractor about how it should be done and whether an inspection applies. The system also needs physical protection for the tank, valves, gauges and piping (s.15(6)), which is a decent reason to put a proper shield in.

Fourth, keep the filter fresh. A filter that's already partly clogged with sludge gives up first when wax arrives. Replacing it at the annual service is normal practice, and it's the cheapest bit of insurance in the whole system. Fifth, keep the tank topped up through the cold weeks if the tag allows it. ## Moving the tank inside: what changes

Indoor placement is the one change that really cuts gelling risk, because the tank and line sit in a space that stays above freezing. It's not free, though. Picture a 1970s bungalow in Torbay with the tank on the north side and a line that runs under a drip edge to the basement wall. Every cold snap, the line is the part that struggles. Moving the tank to a utility room or basement shortens that exposed run to almost nothing.

Moving a tank from outside to inside counts as an alteration under s.2(d), so a licensed inspector has to inspect it (s.12(3)) and the system has to be registered (s.5(1)). Indoor installs also carry their own concerns, like access down stairs and what happens if oil is spilled inside the house. Indoor removal pricing is quoted case by case.

There's a legal bonus. An aboveground steel tank inside a structure with a foundation gets five more years of service life than the same tank outside, in every row of the s.15 table. ## Frequently asked questions

### Can heating oil freeze in my tank?
Oil doesn't freeze like water, but it thickens and clouds in hard cold as the wax in it comes out of solution. That's what people usually mean by gelling. Any water sitting in the tank or filter can freeze as well, so the two problems can look the same.

### Is stove oil less likely to gel than furnace oil?
Stove oil (Type 1) is thinner than furnace oil (Type 2), so it generally handles cold better. Both are named as heating oil in the regulations (s.2(j)). Which one you burn depends on your furnace and your supplier, so ask before you switch anything.

### Can I add something to my tank to stop gelling?
Please don't pour anything in without asking your supplier first. The right product depends on your oil and your system, and it's easy to get wrong in a tank you can't see inside. Your supplier or service tech can tell you what's suitable.

### Does an indoor tank really help?
It helps a lot with cold exposure, since the tank and line stay out of the wind. It comes with trade-offs like access and indoor spill risk, and moving a tank is an alteration that needs a licensed inspector. For a tank that's already near the end of its tag, moving it indoors during a replacement makes the most sense.
