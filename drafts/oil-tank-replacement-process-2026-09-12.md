# The Oil Tank Replacement Process, Step by Step: What Happens on Swap Day

The stick comes up dry again, or the tag you read by torchlight last week says 9-2026, and either way you've made the call: the old tank is coming out. From the photo you send us to the new tag going up on the wall, the oil tank replacement process usually runs about one day, start to finish.

That surprises people. They picture a week of digging, a yard full of gear, the oil truck blocked out of the driveway for days. Mostly it isn't like that. Here's what actually happens, in the order it happens, so you know what to expect and what we need from you at each stage.

It also matters more right now than it did back in July. September and October are when the fall rush starts on the Avalon: first frosts on the way, delivery season ramping up, and everyone who's been putting off a tag decision suddenly deciding at once. Book before that squeeze hits and you get your pick of days. Wait until the first real cold snap and you're competing with everyone else who waited too.

## The photo that starts the oil tank replacement process

Nearly every job starts the same way. You send a photo of the HOST tag, the metal one with the six-digit number and the stamped expiry, and if the tag's already expired or you're not sure what you're looking at, [call (709) 800-3117](tel:+17098003117) before you do anything else. We read the number, ask how old the tank looks, whether it sits inside or out, and give you a straight answer about whether it needs to go now or can wait until spring.

A few other things come up in that first call, too. Is the tank outdoor steel that's spent years taking the salt air and the freeze-thaw straight on, or an indoor tank in a basement that's aged more slowly but is harder to get at? Has it ever run dry, or sat half-empty for a season, which lets condensation build on the floor of the tank where nobody can see it? None of that changes the legal date on the tag, but it does change how urgent the swap is underneath that date.

The tag date isn't a guess and it isn't cosmetic. It's set by the [maximum service life table in the regulations](/blog/how-long-do-oil-tanks-last/), and it runs from the date on the registration, not from the day the tank starts looking rough. A tank can look fine and still be past its legal date. That's the whole reason the tag exists in the first place.

Take a retired teacher in Torbay who checked the tag on her outdoor steel tank with a torch back in August and found it stamped 9-2026. She called in early September rather than waiting for the first cold snap, and by the second week her new tank was in, tagged and registered, well ahead of the fall rush.

## The night before: what we need from you

You don't need to do much, but a few things make the day go faster. Clear the path to the tank.

Move the car if it's blocking the driveway, and if it's later in the season, shovel a route wide enough for a hand truck. Keep the dog inside. That's about it.

Picture a couple closing on a 1980s bungalow off Topsail Road, with a tag that runs out three weeks after their possession date. Their lawyer wanted written confirmation the tank would be replaced before closing, so we scheduled the swap for the week before the deal closed and handed over the new registration papers at the same time the keys changed hands.

If your tank's almost empty, don't top it up the night before. Whatever's left gets pumped out and dealt with as part of the job, and a fuller tank just means more to handle for no reason. If there's anything you need us to sign or leave behind for a lawyer, an insurer or a house sale, say so when you book, not once the truck's already on site.

If the tank sits in a basement or a locked shed, make sure whoever's home knows to leave that access clear too. An indoor swap already takes more care getting the old tank out and the new one in without marking up a stairwell or a doorframe, and a locked door just adds a delay nobody needs on a day that's supposed to be simple.

## What happens on replacement day, step by step

Once the crew arrives, the job runs in a fixed order. Here's the rundown.

| Step | Who does it | What you need to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Written estimate and scheduling | Our office, from your photo of the tag | Send the tag photo, confirm tank size and location |
| Clear a path, move vehicles | You, the night before | Shovel if needed, keep pets inside |
| Pump out remaining oil | Our crew | Nothing, unless we need access to a locked area |
| Disconnect and lift out the old tank | Our crew | Stay clear of the work area |
| Check or replace the stand and pad | Our crew | None |
| Set the new tank, line, filter, vent, whistle and gauge | Our crew | None |
| Inspection, tagging and registration | The installer's licensed inspector | Provide ID or ownership details if asked |
| Handover of paperwork, book first delivery | Our office | Keep the confirmation of registration somewhere safe |

Most of that happens in a single visit. We say usually done in a day and leave it there, because how long any one job takes depends on where the tank sits, whether it's an indoor swap with tighter access, and what the crew finds once the old tank is out of the way.

A homeowner in Kelligrews found that out the hard way, or the good way, depending how you look at it. Her outdoor tank looked rough on top but seemed fine underneath, at least from where she was standing. Once the crew lifted it clear, there was a soft, rusted patch on the bottom that hadn't shown from any angle she could check herself. That's the spot steel fails from, the one you can't see, and it's exactly why the legs and the pad get checked every time too, right along with the tank.

A 200-gallon tank holds about 900 litres; a 100-gallon tank holds about 450. Either way, the fuel line, filter, vent, whistle and gauge all get set fresh on the new tank rather than reused from the old one.

The stand and pad get more attention than people expect. A tank that's tipped even slightly, because a wooden leg rotted or the ground underneath shifted with the frost, puts stress on the outlet and the fuel line in a way that shortens its life no matter what the tag says. If the existing stand is solid, we reuse it. If it's not, that gets fixed before the new tank goes anywhere near it, because setting a new tank on a bad base just starts the same problem over again from day one.

## The inspector's visit: tagging and registering the new system

Only inspection for registration requires a licence under the regulations, so while plenty of people can sell you a tank, only [a licensed inspector](https://www.gov.nl.ca/eccc/env-protection/petroleum/) can tag and register the new one. The installer's licensed inspector handles that part: checking the install, assigning the new tag with its number and stamped expiry, and filing the registration.

Once that's done, you get confirmation of registration, the document lawyers and insurers ask for when you sell the house or renew a policy. Keep it somewhere you'll find again. We wouldn't put our name to a job that skipped this step, and honestly, no legitimate installer here would either.

That confirmation isn't paperwork for its own sake. A lawyer closing a sale wants to see it before the deal completes. An insurer renewing your policy may ask for it before they'll confirm coverage on the house at all. Neither of them wants to hear that the tank is new; they want the registration number that proves an inspector signed off on it.

The [regulations](https://www.assembly.nl.ca/legislation/sr/regulations/rc030060.htm) also say oil can't be delivered to a system without a valid registration label. That's the whole reason the process ends on a tag rather than stopping at a tank. Without one, the next delivery truck that shows up simply won't fill it.

## Booking your first delivery and keeping the paperwork

The old tank leaves the property the same day it comes out, on the same truck. That matters more than it sounds. The regulations give an owner 30 days to empty, dismantle and dispose of a disconnected tank, but when the old one rolls off site the same day, that clock never has a reason to start.

Before the crew leaves, we book your first delivery on the new tank. ## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need to be home for the replacement?
Yes, for at least part of the day. Someone needs to point out the shutoff, answer any questions about where the fuel line runs, and sign for the finished job and the new registration paperwork. You don't need to watch the whole thing happen.

### What happens to the oil left in the old tank?
Whatever's left gets pumped out before the old tank comes off the property. Where the inspector allows it, that oil can be transferred into the new tank; otherwise it's disposed of properly. Either way, don't expect a credit line on the invoice for it, that's not how it works.

### Can the replacement be done in winter?
Yes, and plenty of ours happen in January and February, often because a tag ran out or a tank failed in the cold. Snow and ice just mean the crew needs a cleared path, so shovelling ahead of time matters more in winter than in any other season.

### How soon can I get a delivery after the swap?
As soon as the inspector has tagged and registered the new system, which normally happens the same day the tank goes in. Once that registration is on file, there's nothing stopping the next delivery truck from filling it.
