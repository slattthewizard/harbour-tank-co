# Indoor vs Outdoor Oil Tank: Which Is Better for a Newfoundland Home?

It's minus eight, the wind's coming sideways off the Atlantic, and you're standing in the backyard in your slippers brushing snow off a fill pipe. Meanwhile a house down the street with its tank in the basement never gives it a thought. That's the whole indoor vs outdoor oil tank debate in one picture, and honestly, both sides have a point.

Most Avalon homes have the tank outside, on a stand beside the house. Plenty of older ones have it in the basement or a utility room. If you're about to replace yours, you may be wondering whether it's time to change where it lives. Here's how the two compare, in plain terms.

## What the regulation says about location

The biggest legal difference is time. Under s.15(1) of NLR 60/03, a mild-steel tank gets a longer maximum service life when it sits inside a structure with a foundation. Every row in the table gets five extra years compared to the same tank outside.

Take a 14 gauge tank with an end or top outlet. Outside, it's ten years. Inside, it's fifteen. A 12 gauge tank with an end or top outlet goes from fifteen outside to twenty inside. Bottom outlets follow the same pattern, so a 12 gauge bottom-outlet tank runs twenty years outside and twenty-five inside.

## The case for outside

An outdoor tank is easy to get at. The delivery driver can see it, read the tag, and fill it without tracking through your house. If something goes wrong, the oil is outdoors and the smell isn't in your living room.

Removal is simpler too. Taking out and disposing of an outside domestic tank is $500, and the crew doesn't have to carry a heavy steel tank up a staircase. That's the published figure, and our [oil tank removal page](/oil-tank-removal-st-johns/) goes through it.

The cost of that convenience is weather. Salt air on the Avalon coast, freeze-thaw cycles and wind-driven rain all wear on outdoor steel. Water also condenses inside a half-empty tank and settles on the floor, so steel tanks tend to fail from the bottom, in the one spot you can't see.

Winter adds its own problems. Roof sheds bury fill pipes and vents. Northeasters have knocked tanks off their stands. In the hardest cold, furnace oil can thicken and clog the filter, which is a bigger worry with an exposed line.

Picture a bungalow on the shore side of Conception Bay South where the tank sits against the north wall, right under the roof edge. Snow slides off in March and lands on the vent, and the next delivery gets awkward because the vent is buried. That's the kind of nuisance an outdoor tank brings, and it's mostly fixable with a better location and a bit of shielding.

## The case for inside

An indoor tank is protected from salt spray, snow and ice. It stays warmer, which helps with oil thickening in the cold. It also tends to last longer on paper, for the five-year reason above.

The tradeoffs are the ones you'd expect. If a tank or fitting leaks inside, the oil is in your house, and the smell travels. Our guide on [oil smell in the basement](/blog/oil-smell-in-basement/) is worth a read if you've ever caught a whiff. Access can also be awkward, especially down a narrow staircase, and removal at the end of the tank's life is more work.

Say your tank sits in a utility room of a split-level in Mount Pearl, with the fill pipe running through the wall to the outside. The driver fills from the street, nobody tracks through the house, and the oil stays out of the weather. The catch is that if a leak does start, it starts in the same room as the furnace and the water heater. That's why inside tanks deserve more careful checking than people give them.

The regulations require physical protection for the tank and its valves, gauges and piping, whichever side of the wall it's on (s.15(6)).

## Can you move a tank from outside to inside?

It's a fair question, especially after a frozen fill pipe. The answer is yes, but it isn't a casual job. Moving a tank changes the configuration of the system, and s.2(d) defines that as an alteration. Under s.12(3), an altered system must be inspected by a licensed inspector.

That means the work has to meet CSA B139, the tank manufacturer's instructions and the province's installation manual. Spill precautions have to be taken during the job under s.12(2). You'll need a place inside with a foundation, a suitable path for the fill and vent piping to the outside, and enough room to service it.

Moving the old tank isn't usually worth it. A steel tank's age doesn't reset when you carry it indoors, so you'd be paying for a move and still facing the same tag date. If you're going to the trouble, it often makes more sense to replace and place the new tank where you want it. Ask the installer how the inspector will treat the age of a relocated tank, because we wouldn't want to guess.

## So which one should you choose?

If your current tank is outside and in decent shape, staying outside is usually fine, especially on a lot with decent shelter and room to work. Keep the vent and fill pipe clear, keep the legs and pad solid, and check it before every cold snap. Our [oil tank winter checklist](/blog/oil-tank-winter-checklist/) is the quick version.

If you're on the exposed shore, with salt on the windows and moss on the north wall, a fibreglass tank or an indoor location starts to look attractive. Fibreglass won't rust, and an indoor tank keeps the weather off entirely. Neither is free, and neither is magic.

Whichever side of the wall your tank lives on, a handful of habits pay off. Read the tag twice a year and write the expiry date on your calendar. Keep a stick or gauge reading in your phone notes so you know how fast you burn through a fill in January. Watch the base of the tank for damp patches, because that's where trouble starts.

Outside, brush snow off the vent and fill pipe after every big storm and keep the stand level. Inside, keep the area around the tank clear of boxes and paint cans, and sniff for oil when you come down the stairs. A new smell is information, so don't ignore it. If you're not sure what you're looking at, our [rust on oil tank guide](/blog/rust-on-oil-tank/) helps you sort cosmetic from serious.

None of this replaces a licensed inspector, but it keeps small things small.

If you're buying or selling, location matters because of the tag date. A tank that's inside gets a longer maximum life, which can mean more years on the tag. ## Frequently asked questions

### Is an indoor oil tank safer than an outdoor one?
It depends on what you're worried about. Inside, the tank is sheltered from salt and snow and gets a longer legal life under s.15(1). Outside, a leak is easier to contain and the smell stays out of the house. Neither location is automatically safer, so the condition of the tank and its tag matter more.

### How much longer does an indoor steel tank last?
On paper, five years longer in every row of the s.15(1) table, for the same gauge and outlet. A 14 gauge end-outlet tank goes from ten years outside to fifteen inside. The tank still has to be inspected and registered, so a long tag date isn't a guarantee it's sound.

### Does it cost more to put a new tank inside?
A 200-gallon painted steel tank is $3,200 whether it goes inside or outside. A Roth double-containment tank installed inside is $3,850. Moving the location can add work for piping and access, which is quoted case by case.

### Can I move my existing tank inside myself?
No. Moving a tank is an alteration under s.2(d), and a licensed inspector must inspect an altered system under s.12(3). It also has to be registered before anyone can fill it, so it's a job for an installer who works with a licensed inspector.
