# Can You Leave an Old Oil Tank in Place in Newfoundland? The 30-Day Rule

The heat pump went in three years ago, the oil furnace got disconnected the same week, and the old steel tank out back never left. It still sits on its pad by the fence line, empty and rusting a little more with every northeaster off the harbour. If you're wondering whether you can leave an old oil tank in place once it's out of service, the short answer is no, not for long, and the province is specific about how long "not for long" actually means.

Newfoundland's Heating Oil Storage Tank System Regulations give you a set number of days to decommission a tank once it stops being used, and a much shorter window if there has been a leak. ## No, you cannot simply leave an old oil tank in place

A tank that no longer has oil running through it isn't exempt from the rules. It's not "retired" the moment someone caps the fuel line. The regulations treat disconnection as the start of a clock, not the end of your obligations, and that clock keeps running whether you're thinking about the tank or not.

That catches a lot of homeowners off guard. The HOST tag governs deliveries while a tank is in service, but once it goes out of service a different rule takes over, one most people have never heard of until it's already an issue. 30 days sounds generous until you realize most owners don't think about the tank again until a lawyer or an insurer raises it at the worst possible time.

## The 30-day rule once you disconnect a tank

Section 16(3) of [the regulations](https://www.assembly.nl.ca/legislation/sr/regulations/rc030060.htm) spells out what has to happen within 30 days of disconnecting a heating oil tank. The owner has to empty it, remove any sludge and residual oil, dismantle it, dispose of it properly, remove or cap the piping, and restore the site it sat on. All of it, including the parts that are easy to skip when nobody is checking.

That's a full decommissioning, not a quick drain and a tarp thrown over it. A tank sitting in a yard with the line simply capped and nothing else touched doesn't meet the standard, even if it looks harmless from the road. We cover the whole rulebook, section by section, in our plain-English guide to [Newfoundland oil tank regulations](/blog/newfoundland-oil-tank-regulations/) if you want the full picture beyond this one rule.

The 30 days start on the date of disconnection, not on the date anyone gets around to booking a contractor. A homeowner who capped the line themselves in October and calls a contractor the following spring has been sitting past the deadline the whole winter, whether or not anyone noticed.

## The faster clock after a leak or water intrusion

If the tank didn't simply get quietly disconnected but leaked, or water got in and pushed contamination somewhere it shouldn't be, section 18(5) shortens the whole timeline considerably. You get 10 days for the same decommissioning steps, plus 30 more days for remediation of the site once the tank is gone.

If you find a leak, stop the flow if it's safe to do so, keep people and pets well clear of the area, and don't hose it down or start digging at it yourself. Call the province's 24-hour environmental emergency line and your insurer the same day. The department's [petroleum storage page](https://www.gov.nl.ca/eccc/env-protection/petroleum/) has the current contact details and lays out what they'll want from you.

The short version here: a leak turns a 30-day chore into a 10-day emergency, and the department doesn't stretch that because winter is coming or the ground is frozen.

Leaving an old oil tank in place doesn't save you money. It just moves the cost sideways, adds interest, and hands the decision to someone else's timeline instead of yours.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I leave an empty basement tank in place if it's not connected to anything?
No. An indoor tank that has been disconnected falls under the same 30-day decommissioning rule as an outdoor one, meaning it has to be emptied, cleared of sludge, dismantled, and its piping capped or removed, with the space restored after. Being indoors and out of sight doesn't stop the clock from running.

### Can I keep the old tank and use it for something else?
Not as a fuel tank, and realistically not as much else either. The regulations require a disconnected heating oil tank to be dismantled and disposed of rather than repurposed, so the straightforward answer is to have it removed properly instead of finding it a second life in the yard.

### What if the previous owner left a disconnected tank on the property?
The clock doesn't reset just because the house changed hands. A tank left undecommissioned by a previous owner should be treated as already overdue, since the current owner is the one an insurer or a future buyer's lawyer will be asking about it.

### Do I need proof the tank was removed?
Yes, in practice. Keep whatever documentation the contractor provides showing the tank was decommissioned and disposed of properly, because insurers and lawyers at a future sale will ask for it, and "we had it done a while back" is a weak answer without paperwork behind it.
