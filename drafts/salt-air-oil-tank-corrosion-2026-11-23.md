# Salt Air and Your Oil Tank: Why Coastal Avalon Tanks Rust Faster

You run a finger along the side of your oil tank and it comes away gritty, with a faint white film that wasn't there in the spring. It's the sort of thing you only notice with a torch on a February night. Salt air oil tank corrosion doesn't make a noise. It builds up in the damp, in the places you don't look.

The Avalon coast is hard on outdoor steel. Wind drives salt spray inland, fog leaves a damp film, and the freeze-thaw cycle opens up whatever the weather has loosened. Where the tank sits on the lot makes a bigger difference than most people expect.

## How salt air gets to your tank

Salt doesn't need a storm to reach a tank. Wind carries fine spray well past the shoreline, and fog and wet snow deposit a salty film on whatever they land on. Once it's on painted steel, any chip or scratch lets moisture and salt work at the bare metal underneath.

Add the cycle of a Newfoundland winter. Rain, frost, thaw and frost again. Water that freezes in a scratch widens it, and a wider scratch holds more salt next time. Wind-driven rain pushes moisture into seams, around legs and under any lip where paint is already thin.

We won't give you a corrosion rate or a statistic, because the honest answer is that it varies from lot to lot. A tank on the exposed side of a house in Bay Roberts or Torbay lives a different life than one on the sheltered side of a house in Mount Pearl. What we can tell you is what to look at.

Salt isn't the only way the tank rusts either. Steel tanks fail from the inside too, and from the bottom first. Inside a half-empty tank, condensation puts water on the tank floor, so the worst corrosion sits in the one spot you can't see. ## Where your tank sits on the lot

Siting matters as much as the paint. Look at where your tank sits and ask a few blunt questions.

Is it under the roof's drip line? Meltwater and ice shed from a roof land on the tank, and the roof edge is one of the worst places to put steel. Is it against the wall, where snow drifts and wet leaves pack in and stay damp for weeks? Is it on the windward side, taking the spray first? Is it in a corner that never dries?

Snow piled against a tank is the one you can fix tonight. Packed snow holds salt-laden moisture against the steel for months, hides what's happening to the legs, and puts extra weight on a tank that's already leaning. Clear it by hand and keep a gap around the tank.

## What helps against salt air oil tank corrosion

The goal is simple: keep salt and standing water off the steel for as long as possible. A few habits do most of the work. Take them in rough order of effort.

| Exposure factor | Why it matters | What helps |
|---|---|---|
| Wind-driven salt spray | Leaves a salty film that holds moisture on the paint | Rinse with fresh water on a mild day; touch up chips early |
| Roof drip line above the tank | Constant meltwater and ice on one spot, which breaks down paint and sits in seams | Move the tank or redirect the drip |
| Snow, leaves and debris against the tank | Holds damp salt on the steel for weeks and hides trouble | Clear by hand; keep a gap around the tank |
| Damp, shaded corner | Steel never dries out between storms | Choose a more open spot when you swap |
| Freeze-thaw on the legs and pad | Water in a scratch freezes, widens it and starts again | Keep the base draining; look at the legs each delivery |
| Chipped or scratched paint | Bare metal meets salt directly | Touch up quickly; see the rust guide for what's cosmetic |

Rinsing is worth doing. Use fresh water, gently, on a day above freezing. Keep the water away from the vent, the gauge and the fill pipe, and don't blast it at the seams. If a hose isn't practical, a bucket and a soft cloth work.

Once you see actual rust, the question is how bad it is and whether painting or patching is enough. That's a different question from corrosion exposure, and the [guide to rust on an oil tank](/blog/rust-on-oil-tank/) grades rust from cosmetic to replace-it. Bubbled paint over a wet patch near the bottom is not the same as light surface bloom.

None of this is a substitute for a tank that's within its legal life. Painting a tank doesn't change its age or its tag. The regulation counts a steel tank's life from its registration date, not from how it looks, and the [oil tank life table explained](/blog/how-long-do-oil-tanks-last/) shows how each row works.

## When fibreglass or an indoor tank makes sense on the shore

On an exposed shoreline lot, the best answer is sometimes to change the material or the location, not to fight the weather every year.

A fibreglass tank doesn't rust. It costs $3,850 installed against $3,200 for a 200-gallon painted steel tank, a difference of $650, and both prices include removal of the old tank. The legal life of a fibreglass tank is set by the administrator and capped at 50 years (s.15(2) to (5)), where an outside single-wall steel tank gets between 10 and 20 years depending on gauge and outlet. Fibreglass tags have been seen stamped to 2061.

An indoor tank is the other route. Inside a structure with a foundation, a steel tank gets five extra years in every s.15 row, and it's out of the spray, the drip line and the snow. The Roth double-containment tank installed inside is $3,850. Indoor placement brings its own questions about access, smell and leak risk inside the house, and it has to be done properly, so talk it through before you decide.

The [fibreglass oil tank upgrade](/fibreglass-oil-tank-upgrade-st-johns/) page covers fibreglass and the Roth double-wall in detail, and [steel versus fibreglass](/blog/steel-vs-fibreglass-oil-tank/) compares how each ages. For the shore specifically, if the tank sits where the spray hits, we'd lean toward fibreglass or indoors and let the steel go where the lot is sheltered. If the tank is on the sheltered side and a few years from its tag, steel is still a reasonable call.

## Small habits that slow the damage

You can't stop salt, but you can stop it sitting. A few small habits stretch the life of an outdoor steel tank and cost almost nothing.

Look at the tank every time the truck comes. Turn a torch along the lower third, the seams and the legs, and take the same photo from the same spot each autumn so changes show up. Keep the vent and fill pipe clear after every storm. If the roof sheds snow near the tank, changing where that snow lands can do more than any coat of paint.

Think about the base as well. A tank that sits in a puddle, or on a pad that holds water, rusts from the outside in as well as the inside out. A well-drained footing keeps the legs dry, and dry legs last. Rebuilding a footing can count as an alteration under the regulations (s.2(d)), which brings in a licensed inspector, and it's worth asking about before anyone lifts the tank.

## What the tag can't tell you about corrosion

A tag tells you when the tank must stop being used. It doesn't tell you what's going on at the bottom of the tank, which is where steel usually gives out first. Two tanks with the same tag date can be in very different shape if one has stood in spray for years and the other has been in a sheltered corner.

Say your tank is stamped 8-2028, sits on the shore side, and has bubbled paint at one lower seam. The tag says another year or two. The steel says look closer, and a licensed inspector's judgement, not a rule of thumb, decides whether it's worth waiting.

Where the tag and the steel disagree, trust the steel. A leaking tank has to be reported and emptied, and delivery stops until it's resolved. The department's [petroleum storage page](https://www.gov.nl.ca/eccc/env-protection/petroleum/) explains the HOST program and what owners are expected to do.

Before winter really sets in, look at the lower third of your tank with a torch. Check the seams, the legs and the spot under the gauge. The [pre-winter oil tank checklist](/blog/oil-tank-winter-checklist/) has the rest of the walk-around.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does painting an oil tank stop salt air corrosion?
Paint slows it down by keeping salt and water off the bare steel, as long as the coat stays intact. Chips and scratches let the damage start again, so touching them up early matters. Painting doesn't change the tank's age or its tag date.

### Is fibreglass always the better choice near the ocean?
Fibreglass doesn't rust, so on an exposed lot it removes the main problem. It costs $650 more than steel installed, though, and the right choice depends on your lot, how close the tag is and whether you'd rather go indoors. We'd work it out from a photo of where the tank sits.

### Can I rinse salt off my oil tank?
Yes, with fresh water, gently, on a day above freezing. Keep water away from the vent, gauge and fill pipe. Don't use a high-pressure nozzle on seams.

### How do I know if the inside of my tank is corroding?
You usually can't see it. Water and sludge collect on the tank floor and the steel rusts from the bottom, so the signs show up as water in the oil, a clogged filter or a furnace that cuts out. If any of those turn up on an older steel tank, have it looked at.
