# Landlords and Oil Tanks in Newfoundland: Who's Responsible for What

Say a text lands before seven on a cold morning: the rental is chilly, and the fuel truck left without filling the tank. Whose problem is that? Under the province's regulations, landlord oil tank responsibility comes down to one word, "owner", and it doesn't always mean who you'd guess.

## Landlord oil tank responsibility starts with the word "owner"

The Heating Oil Storage Tank System Regulations (NLR 60/03) put almost every duty on the "owner". The definition turns on who controls the system, not whose name is on the deed. The owner is the person having possessory rights to and care, control or management of the system, and that includes the person's agent (s.2(m)).

The "system" is the tank plus the vent, fill and withdrawal piping and filters, up to where the line connects to the furnace (s.2(n)). So the owner isn't responsible for a steel box. They're responsible for everything between the fill pipe and the furnace.

In a rental, the owner is usually the landlord or the property manager acting for them. "Usually" is doing real work in that sentence. A lease can hand day-to-day care of the system to a tenant, especially in a house rented as a whole, and who has "care, control or management" can vary from one arrangement to the next. If it matters, put it in writing.

You can read the full text of the regulation on the [Assembly's legislation page](https://www.assembly.nl.ca/legislation/sr/regulations/rc030060.htm). For a plain-English walk through the whole thing, see [the Newfoundland oil tank regulations explained](/blog/newfoundland-oil-tank-regulations/).

## What landlord oil tank duties look like in practice

Four duties matter most to anyone who owns an oil-heated rental.

The first is registration. The owner of a new or altered system has to register it, and nobody may use or fill a system installed or altered after the regulations came in unless it's registered (s.5). In practice the installer's licensed inspector tags and registers the system, so a swap leaves you with a new tag.

The second is staying within the tank's legal life. Operating a system that doesn't comply with s.15 is prohibited (s.4(1)). That's the section with the age-and-gauge table, and the date stamped on the tag comes from it.

The third is removal. Within 30 days of disconnecting a tank, the owner has to empty it, remove sludge and residual oil, dismantle and dispose of it, remove or cap the piping and restore the site (s.16(3)). Leaving an old tank to sit isn't an option the regulation recognises.

The fourth is leaks. An owner who believes oil is leaking, or that water is getting into the system, has to inform the department immediately (s.18(1)), and the delivery company too (s.18(3)). After a leak, deliveries stop until the system's integrity has been verified or it has been repaired or replaced to the administrator's satisfaction (s.18(4)).

Here's how the common tasks tend to split. Treat it as a starting point, not a legal opinion.

| Task | Usually the landlord | The tenant's part |
|---|---|---|
| Keep the tank inside its legal life and the tag valid | Yes, as owner (s.4(1), s.15) | Notice the expiry date and flag it early |
| Register a new or altered system | Yes (s.5), through the installer's licensed inspector | Allow access on the day |
| Remove an old tank within 30 days of disconnecting | Yes (s.16(3)) | Keep the area clear |
| Report a suspected leak to the department and the delivery company | Yes (s.18(1), s.18(3)) | Anyone who believes oil is leaking must try to inform the department and the owner (s.18(2)) |
| Order oil and pay for deliveries | Depends on the lease | Depends on the lease |
| Keep the fill pipe and vent clear of snow | Depends on the lease | Often the tenant's day-to-day job |
| Keep people and pets away from a spill | Both | Do this first; don't hose or dig |

## Buying a rental that already has an oil tank

Ownership brings the tank's whole history with it. If you're buying a rental, the tag date and the tank type matter as much as the roof or the wiring, because you'll be the owner the first time a driver reads that tag.

Ask for the registration confirmation, and look at the tag in person. The department's own wording is that the confirmation is often required by an insurance company to insure a home or by a lawyer when selling or buying one. A tank with only a few months left is a negotiating point before closing, not after. The [buyer's checklist for a house with an oil tank](/blog/buying-house-with-oil-tank/) covers what to check, and the same checks apply to a rental.

Ask the seller for the tank's paperwork too. A tag that nobody has looked at in years can leave the next owner a short fuse and a cold tenant.

## What tenants should do about the oil tank

A tenant doesn't carry the owner's duties, but a tenant is usually the first person to notice a problem. The regulations say anyone who believes oil is leaking must try to inform the department and the owner (s.18(2)). That's the baseline: tell the landlord, and the department's 24-hour environmental emergency line if it looks serious.

If the smell of oil is the problem, the first hour matters more than the paperwork. [What to do about an oil tank leak](/blog/oil-tank-leaking-what-to-do/) covers the steps in order, including keeping people and pets away and not hosing or digging.

Day to day, a tenant can help by keeping snow off the fill pipe and vent, looking at the gauge, and telling the landlord when the tank gets low. Reading the tag is easy too. The metal tag has a six-digit number and an expiry in month-year form, and [how to read a HOST tag](/blog/how-to-read-host-tag/) shows what each part means.

A tenant who spots an expiry date within a year can do the landlord a real favour by saying so. Nobody enjoys a cold house in January, and it doesn't take much to avoid one.

## When an expired tag leaves a tenant without heat

Heating oil can't be delivered to a system without a valid, unexpired registration label (s.13(1)(b)(ii)). The driver reads the tag. If it's expired, or the tank looks unsafe, the truck leaves without filling it, and the driver has to report the deficiency to the owner and the administrator right away (s.13(2)).

That means an expired tag isn't an inspection problem anymore. It's a tenant with no heat, in a house that may already be low on oil.

Picture a duplex in Mount Pearl with a tank tag stamped 2-2027, owned by a landlord who lives out of the province. The tank is fine to look at, and nobody checks the date. The first delivery after February is refused, the tenants are cold, and the replacement now has to happen in a hurry in the worst weather of the year.

## Staying ahead of the tag on a rental

The cheapest way to handle a rental tank is to know its expiry date before the tenant does. Put the date in a calendar and aim to decide a year ahead.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is the landlord or the tenant the "owner" of an oil tank?
Under the regulations, the owner is the person with possessory rights to and care, control or management of the system, and that includes their agent. In a rental that's usually the landlord or the property manager, but your lease can shift day-to-day care. We aren't lawyers, so if the lease is unclear, get advice on it.

### Who pays for oil in a rental?
That's set by the lease, not by the oil tank regulations. The regulations deal with the tank system, its registration and what happens if it leaks. Check the lease, and settle it in writing if it says nothing.

### What happens to the tenants if the tag expires?
Heating oil may not be delivered to a system without a valid, unexpired registration label, so the truck leaves without filling the tank. With a full tank the house stays warm for a while, but once it runs low the tenants are without heat. That's why we'd suggest deciding a year ahead of the date rather than waiting for it.

### Does a tenant have to report a leak?
Anyone who believes oil is leaking must try to inform the department and the owner (s.18(2)). The owner then has their own duties to inform the department and the delivery company. Keep people and pets away from the spill and don't hose it or dig, and read the first-hour guide for the rest.
