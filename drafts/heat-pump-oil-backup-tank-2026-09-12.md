# Heat Pump With Oil Backup: Does a Smaller Tank Make Sense?

The heat pump carries the house from April clean through October, quiet and cheap, and most years you forget the oil furnace is even down there. ## Why the tag doesn't care that you barely burn oil anymore

Newfoundland and Labrador's tank rules don't ask how much oil you use. Every registered system carries a metal HOST tag with a six-digit number and an expiry stamped in the department's own MM-YYYY format, something like 7-2028 for a tank good until the last day of July 2028. Under s.15 of NLR 60/03, a mild-steel tank's legal life runs ten to twenty-five years depending on where it sits and how it drains, counted from the registration date, not from how full the tank happens to be.

Section 13(1)(b)(ii) says a system without a valid label can't be delivered to at all, and s.13(2) puts the delivery driver on the hook to report an expired or unsafe-looking tank to the owner and the department right there in the yard. In practice the truck just leaves. A backup tank sitting three-quarters full all summer is no different in the regulation's eyes than one that empties every six weeks in a hard January. The clock runs the same either way.

Picture a couple in Kelligrews who put in a ductless heat pump two summers back and haven't ordered a full delivery since. Their tag reads 3-2027, and if that date passes unnoticed, the driver won't fill the tank in October even though there's still oil sloshing around the bottom of it. ## Sizing a heat pump oil backup: a hundred gallons or two hundred

Once the tag question is settled, the next one is size. A 100-gallon steel tank installed runs $3,100. A 200-gallon steel tank, the size on most Avalon properties already, installed runs $3,200. That's a $100 gap, not the kind of number that should decide anything on its own.

In litres, a 200-gallon tank holds about 900 litres and a 100-gallon tank about 450 litres. For a house that used to burn through six or seven deliveries a winter and now needs one or two, 450 litres is often plenty of reserve.

None of these four options is wrong on its own. The right one depends on how hard the oil furnace still works and how long you plan to stay in the house.

## The case for downsizing: less tank, fresher oil, fewer problems

A tank sitting three-quarters empty for eight months of the year has more airspace than oil, and that airspace breathes with the outdoor temperature. Warm air holds moisture, cool air drops it, and what it drops lands on the coldest surface inside the tank, which is usually the steel floor. Our [article on water and sludge in an oil tank](/blog/water-in-oil-tank/) covers how that happens in detail, but the short version is a smaller tank holding the same volume of oil has less empty space breathing at it all summer.

There's also the fuel itself. Oil sitting in a nearly full 450-litre tank turns over faster than the same volume rattling around in a 900-litre tank built for a house that used to burn through it every six weeks. Fresher oil burns cleaner and gives the filter and the nozzle less to complain about. None of this is dramatic, but for a tank doing light duty now, it adds up over a few winters.

A hundred-gallon tank is also smaller and lighter, which matters on plenty of lots along Topsail Road or in a Paradise subdivision where the side yard between the house and the property line isn't generous. It sits easier under a roof shed in a snowy winter, and it's one less thing crowding a stand that might already be tired.

Take a retired couple in a Paradise bungalow who added a heat pump three years back and now run the furnace maybe six weeks a year. Downsizing from their old 200-gallon tank to a 100-gallon one saved them almost nothing on the invoice, $100, but it freed up floor space by the foundation and gave them a tank sized for what they actually burn.

## The case against downsizing: a cold snap doesn't check your usage log

None of that makes smaller automatically better. A 100-gallon tank holds less reserve, and if a cold snap runs longer than forecast, or a delivery gets pushed by weather, a smaller tank empties sooner. Running out of oil mid-winter means a cold house, a possible air lock in the line, and a service call to get the burner going again. Our [guide to restarting after running out of oil](/blog/ran-out-of-heating-oil/) walks through what that takes.

It's worth saying plainly: burning less oil overall is what shrinks your delivery count, not the tank size. A 100-gallon tank on a house that still burns real volume in January just means more trips for the driver, not fewer. Size the tank to how little oil the house genuinely uses now, not to a hope that a smaller tank will make the furnace sip less.

For a house where the oil furnace is doing more than token backup, an older heat pump that struggles in deep cold, or a big two-storey with a drafty attic, the 200-gallon tank at $3,200 is still the safer bet. ## Fibreglass for a heat pump backup tank you plan to keep for decades

If the heat pump has taken over most of the heating load and the oil furnace is strictly a cold-snap backup, the tank might sit in the yard a long time without ever wearing out on its own schedule. That's exactly where fibreglass or a Roth double-wall tank earns its extra cost. A fibreglass or Roth tank installed runs $3,850, $650 more than steel, and the legal life is set by the administrator rather than capped by gauge and outlet, with tags on record out to 2061.

A steel tank doing light backup duty still corrodes the way any outdoor steel does here: salt air outside, condensation inside, and it doesn't get a pass on the s.15 clock just because it isn't working hard. Our [comparison of steel and fibreglass tanks](/blog/steel-vs-fibreglass-oil-tank/) covers the full trade-off, including what each material is inspected for and what shortens its working life.

## Frequently asked questions

### Will the delivery driver still fill a tank I only use as backup?

Yes, as long as the tag is valid. The regulation cares about the registration and the expiry date, not how many litres you burn through the winter. A backup tank with a current tag gets filled the same as any other tank.

### Does heating oil go bad if it just sits in the tank all summer?

Not the way gasoline does, but a tank sitting mostly full and mostly still is less prone to the water and sludge problems that come with a lot of empty airspace. Turnover matters more than age on its own. If you aren't sure what has settled at the bottom, it's worth checking rather than guessing.

### Can I keep the tank in the yard but disconnect the furnace from it?

You can disconnect the furnace, but the tank doesn't get to just sit there afterward. Once it's disconnected, s.16(3) gives you thirty days to empty it, remove the sludge, dismantle it, and restore the site. Leaving it hooked up but lightly used, with the tag current, is a different situation and is fine.

### Is a hundred-gallon tank enough to get through a real cold snap?

For a house where the oil furnace only carries the coldest weeks, usually yes. About 450 litres covers a good stretch of hard weather. For a house that still leans on oil for weeks at a time, the extra reserve in a 200-gallon tank is the safer call, and the difference in price is only $100.
