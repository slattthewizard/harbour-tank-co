# What a Licensed Oil Tank Inspector Does in Newfoundland (and Why You Need One)

The new tank is sitting on its stand, the old one is gone, and somebody in a hi-vis vest is crouched next to it with a clipboard and a torch. That's the licensed oil tank inspector Newfoundland homeowners hear about all the time but rarely meet. Most people only notice them when a shiny new tag shows up on the tank and a certificate lands in their email.

If you're replacing a tank, or buying or selling a house with one, it helps to know what that person is doing out there and what you're entitled to get from them. It's less mysterious than it sounds.

## Why the inspector is the gatekeeper

Under NLR 60/03, the Heating Oil Storage Tank System Regulations, a tank isn't legal to fill until it's registered. And it isn't registered until a licensed inspector has looked at it. Section 20(1) says only inspection for registration requires a licence, which means nobody else can sign off on your system, no matter how many tanks they've set.

That's why the inspector sits in the middle of every swap. Your installer does the physical work. The installer's licensed inspector checks it, tags it and registers it. Without that step, the fuel truck can't legally put oil in it.

We'll be plain about our own position here. Harbour Tank Co doesn't hold or employ an inspector. The installer works with a licensed inspector, and that's the arrangement the regulation expects. If you're ready to start, you can [get a free written estimate](/#contact) and the inspection is normally part of the install price. ## How someone becomes a licensed inspector

Section 20(2) sets out the licence requirements. In plain words, a person applies to the administrator, holds the certification the administrator requires, and completes extra training and exams. It isn't a weekend course and a stamp.

That matters for two reasons. First, the person tagging your tank has been tested on the rules they're applying. Second, the inspector answers to the administrator, so the sign-off on your tank carries weight with the department.

We won't name anyone, and we won't print a licence number. Your installer should be able to tell you who the inspector is and when they're coming. If an installer goes vague when you ask, that's worth noticing. The [quote line-by-line guide](/blog/oil-tank-quote-whats-included/) shows where the inspection should appear on paper.

## The inspections you might meet

The regulation describes inspections for three situations: a new system, an altered system and an existing one. They're not the same visit, and they don't produce the same paperwork.

Picture a 1980s bungalow in Mount Pearl with a steel tank whose tag reads 3-2027. The owner decides to swap it now rather than wait for the tag to run out. The installer sets a new tank, the inspector inspects it as a new system under s.11, and the owner gets written results plus a new tag.

Now picture a different house, the same street, where the tank is fine but the owner wants the fill pipe moved for a new deck. Moving piping is an alteration under s.2(d), so s.12(3) says a licensed inspector must inspect the altered system. Nothing gets replaced, but the system still has to be looked at and re-registered.

| Inspection type | When it happens | What you get |
|---|---|---|
| New system (s.11) | After a tank is installed fresh | Written results, tag and registration |
| Altered system (s.12) | After tank, piping, filter or protection changes | Written results, updated registration |
| Existing system (s.7) | When an existing tank is registered or checked | Written notice of results and any deficiencies |
| Re-inspection (s.9) | When the administrator orders it | A 30-day window to fix listed deficiencies |

A few things to note about that table. The altered row catches more than people expect, because "alter" in s.2(d) covers adding, removing, repairing or replacing any part of the system, including the filter and the piping. The existing-system row is the one that matters if you're buying: a tank that's never been properly registered can't simply be waved through.

## What you should have in your hands afterwards

The inspector's job isn't finished when the tank is bolted down. After the inspection, you're owed written notice of the results. If there are deficiencies, they have to be listed, and you're the one who has to correct them.

When the system passes, the certificate of registration and the label under s.8 are issued. The label is the metal HOST tag on the tank, with its six-digit number and a stamped expiry. If you want to decode one, our guide to [reading a HOST tag](/blog/how-to-read-host-tag/) walks through it.

Under s.10, licensed inspectors also keep an electronic record of every system's registration and inspection. The department's own wording is that confirmation of registration is often required by an insurance company to insure a home, or by a lawyer or realtor when selling or buying one. Keep your certificate somewhere you'll find it in five years. A photo on your phone counts as a backup, not a replacement.

## What an inspector can and can't tell you to do

This is the part people worry about, and it's covered by the regulation. Section 20(4)(b) says a licensed inspector must recommend only necessary repairs to, or replacement of, a system. They can't pad the list.

That doesn't mean every judgement is cut and dried. A steel tank's legal life comes from the s.15 table, not from how it looks. A tank can look rough and still have years left on its tag, and a clean-looking tank can be at the end of its allowed life. Where the call depends on the inspector's judgement, such as a fitting that's weeping or a leg that's rusted through, the honest answer is that the inspector decides and writes it down.

If the administrator orders a re-inspection under s.9, you get 30 days to fix the deficiencies. That's a fair amount of time in July and a stressful amount of time in a February northeaster, so it pays to deal with listed items quickly. ## Getting ready for the inspector

A little prep makes the visit smoother, and none of it costs money.

Clear the snow from around the tank and the fill pipe and vent. A tank buried under a roof shed's worth of snow is hard to inspect and harder to fill. Have the old paperwork handy, including any previous registration and the installation date if you've got it. If the tank is inside, make sure the route is open and the area is lit.

Pets and kids inside, please. The inspector will want room to work and a clear view of the piping, the filter, the physical protection and the connections up to the furnace. Section 2(n) defines the system as the tank plus all the vent, fill and withdrawal piping and filters, up to where the line connects to the furnace. That's the whole run, tank included.

If something is leaking, the first hour matters more than the inspection. ## Things people get wrong about inspectors

A few common mix-ups are worth clearing up.

First, a tank isn't legal just because the installer says it's fine. Registration by a licensed inspector is what counts. Second, a registered tank isn't registered forever. The tag expires, and s.4(1) makes operating a non-compliant system prohibited. Third, you can't do a bit of piping work and skip the inspector. If it's an alteration, s.12(3) applies.

The last one is the opposite mistake: assuming the inspector will fail a tank for cosmetic reasons. They shouldn't. The regulation points them at what's necessary. If a tank is within its s.15 life and sound, that's a pass.

When you're comparing quotes, ask each installer whether the licensed inspector's inspection, tagging and registration are included and whether you'll get the certificate. ## Frequently asked questions

### Who has to hire the oil tank inspector, me or the installer?
Normally the installer arranges it, because installers work with a licensed inspector who inspects, tags and registers the new system. You don't usually book one separately. Just confirm the inspection and the certificate of registration are part of the quote.

### Can I use a friend who's handy to set the tank and skip the inspector?
You can't skip the inspection. Under s.5(3) nobody may use or fill a new or altered system unless it's registered, and registration needs a licensed inspector. A tank that isn't registered won't get oil from a delivery truck.

### What happens if the inspector finds a problem?
You'll get written notice listing the deficiencies, and you're responsible for correcting them. If the administrator orders a re-inspection, you get 30 days to fix the items. Under s.20(4)(b) the inspector should only recommend repairs or replacement that are necessary.

### Do I need to be home for the inspection?
It's easiest if you are, or if someone can give access to the tank, the piping and the furnace connection. Ask your installer how they handle it.
