# Is There a Rebate for Replacing an Oil Tank in Newfoundland?

The invoice for a new steel tank lands on the counter at $3,200, and you start hunting for a rebate to soften it. If you have been searching for an oil tank rebate in Newfoundland, the honest answer is short: no such program has ever existed, and none is coming. The money that did flow this year was never for a tank. It was for getting rid of oil heat altogether.

That distinction matters more than it sounds like it should. It's a common question around Kelligrews and everywhere else on the Avalon: can the federal heat pump money go toward "just the tank part" of a job? It couldn't, and since the end of July the federal intake has been closed anyway. If you're budgeting for a straight swap, here's what actually existed, what it paid for, and what that leaves you paying yourself this fall.

## No, there's no rebate for swapping one oil tank for another

Every grant program that has touched heating oil in this province, federal or provincial, has paid people to stop using it. Not one has ever paid a homeowner to replace an old oil tank with a new oil tank. That includes steel, fibreglass and the Roth double-wall tanks we install. If a contractor tells you otherwise, ask them to point to the program in writing, because we have never seen one.

It's a fair question to ask, because the logic sounds close. A leaking, expired tank is an environmental problem, and government money often follows environmental problems. But the programs that existed were built around emissions, not tank safety. A tank swap keeps a house burning oil. A heat pump conversion stops it. That's the line the money was drawn around, and it explains almost everything else in this article.

For the real cost of a compliant replacement, whichever tank you land on, see our [tank replacement cost guide](/oil-tank-replacement-cost/). It carries every figure we quote, with nothing left vague.

## Where the rebate money in Newfoundland went

Two programs did real money here, and both are now closed to new applicants as of this fall.

The federal Oil to Heat Pump Affordability program gave eligible lower-income, oil-heated households an upfront payment of up to $10,000 toward installing a heat pump. Intake closed on 31 July 2026. Anyone already approved has until 31 January 2027 to finish the work and upload their documents, and the program is transitioning over to Environment and Climate Change Canada after that. If you're reading this hoping to still get in the door, you cannot, at least not through this version of the program.

The provincial takeCHARGE Oil to Electric program ran a general stream that covered installs and invoices dated between 1 April 2023 and 31 March 2026. That stream is now closed too. A second stream, for low- and moderate-income households, keeps running to 31 March 2027, though we aren't going to quote a dollar figure for it here because the province hasn't published one we can verify. If that stream might apply to your household, ask takeCHARGE directly for the current eligibility rules. For the federal side, the [federal Oil to Heat Pump Affordability page](https://natural-resources.canada.ca/energy-efficiency/homes/canada-greener-homes-initiative/oil-heat-pump-affordability-program/24775) still lists the closing date and the document deadline.

Picture a retired couple in Torbay who converted their 1980s bungalow to a ducted heat pump last year under the old general stream. They got real money for that. If they called today asking for the same deal on a tank replacement, the answer would be no, because that program was never built to cover a tank in the first place.

## What replacing your tank costs without a rebate

With the grant question settled, the number you need to plan around is the one for the tank itself. A 200-gallon (about 900-litre) painted steel tank, installed, with the old one removed, runs $3,200. A fibreglass tank installed comes to $3,850, and a Roth double-containment tank installed indoors is the same, $3,850. If you're on a heat pump already and only need a smaller backup tank, the 100-gallon (about 450-litre) steel option installed is $3,100, $100 less than the 200-gallon size.

Removal on its own, for an outdoor domestic tank with nothing new going in its place, is $500. An indoor or basement removal has no published figure, so we'll say plainly: that one gets quoted case by case, and we aren't going to invent a range to fill the gap.

## The rebate you can give yourself: choosing fibreglass

If there's a rebate hiding in this decision anywhere, it isn't a government cheque. It's the difference between a tank you replace again in 10 to 15 years and one you don't touch again for decades. Fibreglass and Roth tanks get a service life set by the administrator under the regulations, capped at 50 years, and fibreglass tags have been seen stamped out to 2061. Steel, by comparison, tops out at 25 years even in the best conditions, an inside tank with a bottom outlet on 12 gauge steel.

The extra cost for that difference is $650, the gap between the $3,200 steel tank and the $3,850 fibreglass or Roth tank. Spread across a tag that might run three or four times longer than steel's maximum, that's the closest thing to a subsidy you'll find in this decision, and it's one you control yourself rather than one that depends on a federal intake window. ## If you converted under a grant and still have the old tank

Here's the part people miss. If you did take a heat pump grant, provincial or federal, and disconnected your oil tank as part of that work, the province doesn't care that a rebate paid for the new system. The old tank is still your responsibility under the regulations, and the clock on it started the day you disconnected it.

Section 16(3) gives you 30 days from disconnection to empty the tank, remove the sludge and residual oil, dismantle it, dispose of it, cap or remove the piping, and restore the site. That rule doesn't bend for a tank that's technically "done" because a heat pump replaced it. Take a typical case: a homeowner in Mount Pearl who converted last year and left the disconnected steel tank sitting against the fence through the winter is, as of this fall, well past that window and exposed if an inspector or a future buyer's lawyer ever asks about it.

Outdoor removal on its own is $500, the same figure whether the tank was disconnected for a heat pump or simply aged out. ## Frequently asked questions

### Is there a tax credit for installing a new oil tank?

Not that we can verify. No federal or provincial program we have found pays a credit or rebate for replacing an oil tank with another oil tank, tax credit or otherwise. If you come across one, ask to see it in writing before you rely on it, because we have not.

### Can I still apply to the federal Oil to Heat Pump Affordability program?

New applications closed on 31 July 2026. If you were already approved before that date, you have until 31 January 2027 to finish the work and upload your documents. Beyond that, the program is transitioning to Environment and Climate Change Canada, so check its page directly for whatever comes next.

### Does my oil delivery company give a credit toward a new tank?

We aren't going to guess at another company's policy, so ask your supplier directly rather than assuming either way. What we can tell you is that no government program covers this, and we have never seen a delivery company advertise one either.

### What's the cheapest legal way to replace an oil tank in Newfoundland?

Without a rebate, the lowest published figure is the 100-gallon steel tank installed at $3,100, suited to homes already running a heat pump that only need oil for backup. A full 200-gallon steel swap is $3,200. Fibreglass costs more up front at $3,850 but buys a legal life several times longer than steel's maximum, so cheapest today isn't always cheapest over the life of the tag. Get a written estimate for both before you decide, since the right answer usually comes down to how long you plan to stay in the house.
