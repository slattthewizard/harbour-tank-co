# How Old Is My Oil Tank? Ways to Date It When the Tag Is Missing

You crouch beside the tank with a torch, brush the snow off the side, and find a bare patch of steel where the tag should be. Maybe it rusted through, maybe it was painted over years ago, maybe it fell off in a northeaster. Either way, "how old is my oil tank?" just went from a curiosity to a real question, because the fuel truck reads that tag before it fills anything.

The good news is that there are several places to look, and some of them cost nothing. The less good news is that the regulations have a firm view on tanks whose age can't be proven. We'll go through both, so you know what to dig out of the filing cabinet before you call anyone. ## Start with the HOST tag if you can

Every registered tank system in the province carries a metal HOST tag with a six-digit number and a stamped expiry in MM-YYYY form. The department's own example is that "7-2028" means the tank may not be used after 31 July 2028. Our [HOST tag guide](/blog/how-to-read-host-tag/) covers how to read it, so we won't repeat that here.

One thing worth saying plainly. The tag gives you an expiry, not a birthday. The expiry comes from the legal life under s.15 counted from the effective date on the registration, and that life depends on gauge, outlet and whether the tank is inside or outside. Our [life table guide](/blog/how-long-do-oil-tanks-last/) explains the maths. Working backwards from an expiry to a manufacture date is guesswork unless you know the tank's class.

If the tag is there but dirty, wipe it with a rag before you give up. Salt film and old paint hide a lot of stamping, and a phone camera with the flash on will often pick up numbers your eyes can't. If it's genuinely gone, move on to the next section.

## How old is my oil tank without a tag: where else to look

You're looking for any paper or stamp that carries a date. Start with the tank itself. Many steel tanks have a manufacturer's plate or a stamped mark on the body, near the top or on an end. It may be under paint, so check with a torch held at a low angle to the surface.

Then go indoors. Installation invoices are the best proof you can have, and s.6(3) of the regulations says the age of an existing system is set from proof of the installation date. Home purchase documents sometimes mention the heating system, and a past inspection report may list the tank and its age. If you bought the house, the previous owner might remember when the tank went in, even if all they remember is the year the old one failed.

The last stop is the inspector's side of things. Licensed inspectors keep electronic records (s.10 of the regulations). If a fragment of the six-digit tag number survives, or you know which company installed or serviced the system, ask whether a licensed inspector can look the system up. That's their call, and it doesn't always lead anywhere, but it costs a phone call.

Photograph everything you find before you touch it. A plate that's legible in daylight can flake off when you rub it, and a photo of a faint stamp is easier to enlarge than the stamp itself. Write down where on the tank each mark sits, too. An inspector or installer will want to know whether a number is on the body, the end or a fitting.

Be careful with one trap. A date stamped on a fitting, a gauge or a filter is the date that part was made, and it can be much newer than the tank, because parts get swapped over the years. Only a mark on the tank body tells you about the tank.

## A quick reference for dating a tank

Not every clue is equal. This table sorts them by how much weight they'll carry when someone has to decide your tank's age.

| Where to look | What it tells you | How reliable |
| --- | --- | --- |
| HOST tag | Registration number and expiry date | High, if it's readable and still on the tank |
| Manufacturer's plate or stamp on the tank | Maker, gauge and sometimes a manufacture date | Good, but the date may be faint or missing |
| Installation invoice or work order | The date the system went in | High, because it's proof of installation |
| Home purchase documents or inspection report | A rough age or a listed tank | Moderate; it depends on what the report says |
| Previous owner | When the tank was installed or replaced | Low to moderate; it's memory, not paper |
| Inspector's electronic records | Past registration details for the system | Useful if a record exists; the inspector decides |
| Rust and condition | A rough sense of wear | Low; condition doesn't set legal age |

Notice what's at the bottom. A tank that looks old might be fine, and one that looks clean might be past its date. Appearance can be a reason to inspect, but it doesn't set legal age.

## What the regulations say about a tank of unknown age

Under s.6(3), the age of an existing system is set from proof of the installation date or, failing that, the tank's manufacture date. A tank whose age can't be determined is considered to be of unknown age and can't be registered. You can read the wording on the [regulation page](https://www.assembly.nl.ca/legislation/sr/regulations/rc030060.htm).

Now follow that through. Heating oil can't be delivered to a system without a valid, unexpired registration label (s.13(1)(b)(ii)). So a tank that can't be registered can't get a delivery. And operating a system that doesn't comply with s.15 is prohibited (s.4(1)).

In practice the driver reads the tag, and if it's missing or expired, the truck leaves without filling. If that's your situation, our [refused delivery guide](/blog/oil-delivery-refused/) explains what happens next. We'll be straight about one thing. Whether a particular inspector accepts a particular piece of paper as proof is their judgement, and we can't promise it. Ask them before you spend a weekend hunting through boxes.

## Two Avalon examples of how this plays out

Picture a 1980s bungalow in Mount Pearl where the tag is gone and the owners have the original build documents but nothing about the tank. The tank may be the original, or it may have been swapped in 1998. With no invoice and no readable plate, there's no proof of installation or manufacture date, so the system is treated as unknown age. Nobody's going to register that, which means no deliveries.

Now say a split-level on the same street has a tank with the manufacturer's plate still readable under a few layers of paint, plus an invoice from the previous owner's file. That's enough to set an age, and the owners can plan a swap around the tag date instead of around a panic. That's the moment to ask for the paperwork in writing, or to talk about replacing the tank as part of the deal, because a lawyer or insurer who wants confirmation of registration has nothing to look at. Winter makes it worse, since nobody wants to find out in January that the first delivery will be refused.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I just look at the tank and guess its age?
You can guess, but a guess won't register a system. Rust and wear tell you the tank has been around, not when it went in. The regulations set age from proof of the installation date or the manufacture date (s.6(3)).

### What if the tag fell off but the tank is in good shape?
Good shape doesn't change the paperwork problem. If nobody can show an installation date or a manufacture date, the tank may be treated as unknown age and can't be registered. Ask a licensed inspector what they'd accept as proof before you decide.

### Does a missing tag mean I can't get oil?
Delivery needs a valid, unexpired registration label (s.13(1)(b)(ii)). With no label, the driver has no way to confirm it, and the truck leaves without filling. Sort the tag out before the next delivery is due.

### Where do I find an installation invoice?
Check your own files first, then any home purchase package and the previous owner's records. If a company installed or serviced the tank, ask them for a copy. A date on an invoice is the strongest proof you can have.
