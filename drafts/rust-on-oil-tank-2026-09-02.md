# Rust on Your Oil Tank: Surface Stain or Time to Replace?

That orange streak down the side of the tank wasn't there in the fall. Now the snow's finally off the pad and you're standing in the backyard in Paradise wondering whether you're looking at a stain or a bill.

Here's the honest answer. Rust on oil tank steel is sometimes nothing and sometimes the last warning you'll get before a leak. The trick is telling the two apart, and most of the time you can do that in ten minutes with a rag and a flashlight.

This guide covers what each kind of rust means, how to check without making things worse, what your HOST tag says about it, and what replacing a rusty tank costs around St. John's and the Avalon.

## Why rust on an oil tank is a bigger deal on the Avalon

A residential heating oil tank is a mild-steel box holding around 900 litres of fuel, usually sitting on a stand a couple of feet off the ground. Steel and water don't get along. Add salt air off the harbour, a winter of freeze and thaw, and drizzle that blows sideways for weeks, and the outside of that box is under attack from the day it's installed.

The inside has it worse. Every time the tank sits half empty through a mild spell, moisture condenses on the cold steel above the oil line. That water sinks to the bottom because it's heavier than oil, pools there, and eats the floor of the tank from the inside out. That's why tanks almost always fail at the bottom, in the one spot you can't see.

The province doesn't leave this to guesswork. Under the [Heating Oil Storage Tank System Regulations, 2003](https://www.assembly.nl.ca/legislation/sr/regulations/rc030060.htm), every registered tank in Newfoundland and Labrador carries a HOST tag with an expiry date. That date is based on the tank's steel gauge, outlet position and location, not on how it looks. A tank can be gleaming and still be illegal to fill, and a scruffy one can have years left. We'll come back to that.

One thing before you go further. If the rust you found is wet, if there's a bead of oil sitting in it, or if you can smell fuel near the tank, stop reading and call [(709) 800-3117](tel:+17098003117). That line's answered 24 hours a day, and a weeping tank in a St. John's winter isn't something to sleep on.

## The four kinds of rust you'll see on a rusty oil tank

Not all rust is the same rust. Here's what each type looks like and what it usually means.

**1. Surface bloom.** A light orange haze, or a few freckles where the paint's been scratched by a shovel or worn by the fill hose. Wipe it with a rag and the metal underneath is smooth and solid. This is cosmetic. It'll spread if you ignore it, but it isn't hurting the tank today.

**2. Run-off streaks.** Rust-coloured drips running down from a fitting, the gauge, the vent, or the fill pipe. Nine times out of ten this is the fitting rusting, not the tank. It still deserves a look, because a weeping fitting can be leaking a little oil too.

**3. Scale and flaking.** Rust thick enough to lift off in layers, like the bottom of an old wheelbarrow. When it comes away under a fingernail, the steel underneath has lost thickness. This is where "stain" turns into "structural." A 14-gauge tank wall is only about 2 millimetres thick to begin with, and it doesn't have much to give.

**4. Pitting and weeping.** Small dark craters in the steel, sometimes with a damp ring around them, sometimes with a drop of oil sitting right in the pit. This is the tank corroding through. A weeping pit on the bottom is a leak that hasn't found its way out yet, and it will.

The first two you can handle with a wire brush and a can of rust paint. Our post on [painting an oil tank to slow rust](/blog/rust-on-oil-tank/) covers how to do it properly. The last two mean the tank is on borrowed time, no matter what the tag says.

## A ten-minute rust check you can do yourself

You don't need tools for this. Grab a flashlight, a clean rag, and a flattened cardboard box.

**Look at the legs and the pad first.** Rust at the base of the legs means water's been pooling there. Check the tank's still sitting level. One that's tilted away from the outlet keeps water at the far end with no way out.

**Wipe the worst spot.** Rub the rustiest patch with the rag. Smooth and the same colour as the rest of the tank means surface rust. Rough, flaky, or dotted with tiny dark spots means pitting.

**Press, don't poke.** Push firmly on the rusty area with your thumb. Solid steel doesn't move. If it flexes, dents, or crackles, stop right there and don't reach for a screwdriver. People have put a hole in a weak tank floor trying to "test" it, and ended up with 600 litres of oil on the lawn.

**Slide the cardboard under the tank.** Leave it overnight and check it in the morning. Even a spot the size of a loonie means the tank or a fitting is weeping.

**Sniff around the fittings.** Fresh heating oil has a sharp, unmistakable smell. If you catch it near the bottom outlet, the filter, or the seam along the bottom, that's your answer.

**Read the tag.** Write down the six-digit number and the expiry, stamped as month and year, something like 04-2027. You'll want it for the next section.

**Know when to stop.** End the DIY check if you find wet rust, a drop of oil, flaking on the bottom half, or an expired tag. At that point you need a licensed inspector, not a second opinion from the neighbour.

## What the HOST tag says about a rusty oil tank

Rust tells you how the tank's doing. The tag tells you how long the province will let you use it. Those are two different questions, and you need answers to both.

Every registered tank system in NL carries a metal HOST tag with a six-digit number and an expiry stamped in MM-YYYY form. A tag reading "7-2028" means the tank can't be used after July 31, 2028. That expiry comes from section 15 of the regulations, and it depends on the gauge of the steel, where the outlet is, and whether the tank is inside or outside.

For a plain single-wall steel tank, the maximum service life works out like this:

| Tank type | Outside | Inside a building with a foundation |
|---|---|---|
| 14-gauge, end or top outlet | 10 years | 15 years |
| 12-gauge, end or top outlet | 15 years | 20 years |
| 14-gauge, bottom outlet | 15 years | 20 years |
| 12-gauge, bottom outlet | 20 years | 25 years |

Fibreglass and double-wall tanks get a much longer life set by the administrator, capped at 50 years. Fibreglass tags have been seen stamped out to 2061.

Two things follow from that table. First, those numbers are maximums, not guarantees. A badly rusted tank with years left on the tag can still be failed by the inspector or refused by the driver, which is what happened to Gerald. Second, once the tag expires, looks don't matter. Section 13 says heating oil can't be delivered to a system without a valid registration label, and section 4 says you can't operate one. The prettiest tank in Conception Bay South is a lawn ornament once the date passes.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I just paint over rust on my oil tank?

Yes, if it's surface rust with solid steel underneath. Wire-brush the loose rust off, wipe the area clean, and apply a rust-inhibiting metal paint in two thin coats. Don't paint over flaking, pitting, or anything damp, because paint hides the problem without fixing it, and the inspector will want to see bare metal anyway.

### Will a delivery driver refuse to fill a rusty oil tank?

They can, and on the Avalon they do. Under the provincial regulations a driver who spots a deficiency has to report it, and a soft or weeping tank counts. An expired HOST tag is an automatic refusal no matter how the tank looks. If you've been refused, call the 24/7 line and treat it as urgent, because there's no second delivery coming until the tank is replaced and tagged.

### Does rust mean my oil tank is leaking?

Not by itself. Surface rust and streaks from fittings are common on tanks with years of safe life left. What signals a leak is wet rust, a drop of oil in a pit, a stain on the pad or floor, or a fresh fuel smell near the bottom of the tank. If you see any of those, stop testing it yourself and get a licensed inspector to look at it.

### Is a fibreglass tank worth the extra money if rust is the problem?

For most homes in St. John's, yes. A fibreglass tank runs $3,850 installed versus $3,200 for painted steel, and it can't rust from the salt air outside or from condensation inside. It also gets a much longer HOST tag, so you're not repeating this whole exercise in 10 to 15 years. If you plan to stay in the house, the extra $650 usually pays for itself in one avoided replacement.
