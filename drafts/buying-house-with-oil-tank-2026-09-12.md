# Buying a House With an Oil Tank in Newfoundland: What to Check Before Closing

You're standing in a stranger's basement with your phone's flashlight, reading a small metal tag stapled to the side of an oil tank, and none of the numbers stamped on it mean anything to you yet. Closing is three weeks out and your lawyer just emailed asking for "confirmation of registration."

Buying a house with an oil tank in Newfoundland means that tag matters as much as the roof or the furnace, maybe more, because it can decide whether a truck will fill the tank the week you move in. Skip it and you could own a house with a tank nobody can legally deliver to.

This is the walk-through most buyers never get shown: what the tag tells you, what your lawyer and your insurer actually need, what to check with your own eyes before the offer goes firm, and what's fair to ask the seller for. ## Why the tag matters more than the roof when you're buying a house with an oil tank

Every registered oil tank in Newfoundland and Labrador carries a HOST tag: a six-digit number and an expiry stamped in month-year form. Under the [Heating Oil Storage Tank System Regulations](https://www.assembly.nl.ca/legislation/sr/regulations/rc030060.htm), a delivery driver isn't allowed to fill a tank without a valid, unexpired registration label, and if the tag is expired or the driver doesn't like what they see, the truck leaves without a drop going in. That rule doesn't pause for a closing date.

So the question isn't just "does the house have oil heat." It's "does the tag on that tank outlive your first winter in the house." A tag that expires the month after you move in is a problem you inherit on day one, not a future homeowner's problem. A tag with several years left is one less thing to think about.

None of this shows up on the listing sheet. It shows up on a metal tag, usually on the side or shoulder of the tank, and most buyers walk right past it.

## Reading the tag before your offer goes firm

The tag itself, and how to read the number and the expiry stamped on it, is covered step by step in our guide on [how to read a HOST tag](/blog/how-to-read-host-tag/). Bring that page up on your phone at the showing. It takes about two minutes once you know where to look.

What you're checking for at this stage is simple: does the expiry date fall before or after your expected closing, and how much runway is left after that. A tank tagged to expire six months after closing gives you breathing room to plan a swap on your own schedule. A tank tagged to expire before closing, or within the first winter, changes the negotiation entirely, because you'd be buying a house that can't legally take a delivery of oil until someone deals with the tank.

If the seller can't produce a tag at all, treat that the same as an expired one. No tag usually means no valid registration, and that's a "before closing," not a "settle in first," conversation.

## What confirmation of registration means for your lawyer and your insurer

The phrase in your lawyer's email has an official meaning, and the [department's petroleum storage page](https://www.gov.nl.ca/eccc/env-protection/petroleum/) spells it out. The province's own wording is that confirmation of registration is often required by an insurance company to insure a home, or by a lawyer or realtor when a home is being bought or sold. It's the paper trail that says the tank on the property is legally registered and tagged, not merely sitting there.

Only a licensed inspector can carry out that registration and issue the confirmation, under the rules governing who's allowed to inspect a heating oil system. A seller, a fuel supplier, or a general contractor can sell you a tank, but none of them can register it. If the confirmation doesn't exist yet, that's the thing to chase before you waive conditions, not after.

For the fuller picture of what the regulations require and why, our plain-English rundown of [Newfoundland's oil tank rules](/blog/newfoundland-oil-tank-regulations/) covers the registration system, the delivery ban and the removal deadlines in one place.

Ask the seller for two documents: the confirmation of registration itself, and the install year of the tank if it's on file anywhere. A seller who bought the house recently may not have either. That's not unusual, and it isn't necessarily a red flag on its own. It just means someone needs to track it down, or get the tank looked at fresh.

## Walking the tank yourself before the offer goes firm

You don't need to be a tradesperson to do a useful walk-around. Bring a flashlight and give yourself five minutes.

Start at the bottom, because that's where these tanks fail. Steel tanks corrode from the inside out along the floor, where condensation collects under a half-empty tank, in the one spot you can't see without getting down at ground level and looking up and under. Rust weeping from underneath, a dark stain on the pad, or a smell of fuel near the base are all things to flag before you go firm, not after the movers show up. Our detailed guide on [checking rust on an oil tank](/blog/rust-on-oil-tank/) walks through which kind of rust is cosmetic and which kind isn't.

Then check the legs and the pad. They should be level and solid, not sinking into the yard or cracked underneath. Follow the copper fuel line from tank to furnace and look for kinks, green corrosion, or a splice that looks improvised. Check that the vent and the whistle cap are clear and the gauge on top moves.

Take a typical Kelligrews sale: the buyer's home inspector noted "moisture near the oil tank" in three words on page nine of the report and moved on to the attic. The buyer crouched down with her phone light afterward and found rust weeping from the underside of a tank with no tag anywhere on it. That single look changed the offer.

## What to negotiate before you close on a house with an oil tank

Once you know what you're dealing with, the negotiation usually comes down to one of two things: get the seller to deal with the tank before closing, or negotiate a credit and deal with it yourself right after.

| What to check | Where to look | Red flag | What to ask for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tag and expiry date | Side or shoulder of the tank | Expired, missing, or scratched illegible | A copy of the registration and the install year |
| Confirmation of registration | Seller's paperwork, or their lawyer | None exists or nobody can find it | Written confirmation before closing |
| Rust and staining | Base of the tank, the pad underneath | Weeping rust, dark stains, oil smell | A written estimate before you close |
| Legs and pad | Full circle around the tank | Sinking, cracked, or badly rusted stand | Repair or a new install as a condition |
| Fuel line and fittings | Copper line, tank to furnace | Kinks, green corrosion, improvised splices | A quote for a full line swap |
| Tank age vs. house age | Compare to house records if available | Tank clearly older than the furnace or roof | A credit toward a new tank |
| Disconnected tank on the property | Basement corner, yard, old carport | Sitting unused with no removal paperwork | Proof of proper decommissioning |

Still, the tank is the original 200-gallon steel unit (about 900 litres), painted but visibly tired, and it's already outlived a lot of the other original mechanicals in the house. Rather than wait for the tag to run out mid-winter, the buyers negotiate a credit against the cost of swapping it for a Roth double-wall tank at $3,850 installed, which trades a shorter legal window for a tag that could run for decades. A plain 200-gallon steel replacement, for comparison, runs $3,200 installed; a 100-gallon unit (about 450 litres), sized for a heat-pump backup home, is $100 less again, at $3,100. There's no rebate program that pays toward another oil tank, and there never has been. The federal and provincial programs that existed were for switching away from oil entirely, and both have closed their general intake. Don't let anyone tell you a grant will cover this.

For homes where the tank looks fine, has a tag with years left, and clears the walk-around, the fair move is often to do nothing and revisit it closer to the expiry date. ## If the seller already disconnected a tank

Sometimes the tank isn't in service at all. The house switched to a heat pump a few years back, or the furnace was replaced, and the old steel tank is still sitting in the basement corner or against the back wall, empty and forgotten.

The regulations give an owner thirty days after disconnecting a tank to empty it, remove sludge and residual oil, dismantle it, dispose of it properly, cap or remove the piping, and restore the site. If a leak or water intrusion was involved, that window shrinks to ten days, plus another thirty for cleanup. A tank that's been sitting disconnected for two years with none of that done isn't following the rule, whoever's name is on the title.

Take a typical Topsail Road split-level in Conception Bay South: the sellers added a heat pump two winters ago and left the old tank standing empty against the basement wall, meaning to get to it eventually. It's the buyer's business only in the sense that a rusting steel box with old sludge in the bottom, sitting in a basement they're about to own, isn't nothing. Ask for proof it'll be removed before closing, or a credit that covers it. Removal and disposal of an outdoor domestic tank runs $500; an indoor or basement removal is quoted case by case once someone's looked at the access. Our [tank removal and disposal page](/oil-tank-removal-st-johns/) covers the thirty-day rule end to end, and our post on [removal cost, indoor versus outdoor](/blog/oil-tank-removal-cost/) lays out what changes the number.

Whoever disconnected the tank owns that thirty-day clock. But you're the one who has to live with a rusting tank in the basement if nobody closes it out, so get it in writing rather than taking a verbal promise at the walk-through.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I get insurance on a house with an expired tag?

Often, yes, but expect your insurer or broker to ask about it at binder time, and an expired tag can slow things down while it gets sorted out. It's rarely a flat refusal, but it's not something to discover the week before closing either. Ask your broker directly before you waive conditions, and get the tank swap scheduled if the tag is a problem.

### Who pays for the new tank?

Whatever you and the seller agree to in the purchase negotiation. Some sellers replace an old or expired tank before closing; others give a credit against the cost and let the buyer arrange it after taking possession. No government program pays toward another oil tank, so budget it as either the seller's cost, a credit, or a move-in expense you cover yourself.

### Does a home inspector check the oil tank?

A general home inspection isn't a HOST inspection, and most home inspectors will flag visible issues like rust or a missing tag without pronouncing on the tank's remaining legal life. Only the installer's licensed inspector can register a tank and issue confirmation of registration. Treat a note in the inspection report as a reason to look closer yourself, not the final word on the tank's condition.

### What if there's no tag at all?

No tag usually means the tank was never registered, or the registration lapsed with nothing renewed, and either way a delivery driver can refuse to fill it under the regulations. Treat a missing tag exactly like an expired one: get a written estimate and a plan in place before you close, not after a truck turns around in the driveway on move-in week.
