# Oil Tank Storm Damage: Checking Your Tank Once the Wind Dies Down

The northeaster finally drops around four in the morning. You check the power, then the furnace, and then you stand at the back window with a torch trying to see whether the tank is still where it was yesterday.

Oil tank storm damage is easy to miss in the dark and expensive to miss in daylight. A tank that's shifted a few centimetres on its legs, a vent plugged solid with ice, a line bent by a slab of snow off the roof: all of it looks like nothing until the furnace quits or the truck won't fill.

If you can smell oil, or the tank has moved, jump to the [expired and damaged tank emergency page](/expired-oil-tank-emergency-st-johns/) or call [(709) 800-3117](tel:+17098003117). Otherwise, put your boots on and walk the tank in this order.

## Start with the walk-around, not the furnace

Before you touch anything, look from a few metres back. Is the tank sitting level? Does it look the same height at both ends? A lean you can see from the porch is a lean that matters.

Then get closer, and use your eyes and nose before your hands. Wet patches on the snow or ground under the tank, a dark stain on the pad, a smell of fuel that wasn't there yesterday: these tell you more than any gauge does. If you're on the Avalon coast in a wind event, assume there's more going on than you can see, because the weather that blew your tank around also packed snow into every gap.

Don't lean on the tank, don't shove the legs, and don't climb on a snow-covered stand. A steel tank full of oil is heavy, and a frozen stand doesn't forgive a slip.

## What to look at, in order

A calm, set sequence beats a panicky glance. Work through it the same way every time.

1. The tank and its legs. Level, square on the pad, no daylight under a foot that's meant to be sitting down.
2. The shell. Dents, gouges or scraped paint where something hit it.
3. Wet spots and smell. Check the base, the seams and the fittings.
4. The vent and the fill pipe. Clear of snow, ice and anything the wind blew in.
5. The line to the furnace. Not crushed, kinked or pulled tight.
6. The gauge. Intact, and reading something sensible.

The regulations also require physical protection for the tank and its valves, gauges and piping (s.15(6)). In plain terms, a shield, a guard or a roof structure that was doing its job before the storm should still be doing it after. If a shed collapsed onto the tank or ice has taken out the protection, count that as damage even if the tank looks fine.

Our [pre-winter checklist](/blog/oil-tank-winter-checklist/) is the guide for getting ready before the weather hits. This page is for the morning after.

## What you see, what it means, and what to do next

| What you see | What it means | Next step |
|---|---|---|
| Tank level, legs firm, no wet spots | Probably fine | Check the vent and gauge, then recheck after the next big wind |
| Tank leaning or a leg bent or sunk | The footing has failed or is failing | Don't refill it, don't push it back, call a licensed installer |
| Oil smell, wet staining or a sheen on snow | Possible leak | Follow the leak steps below and report it |
| Vent or fill pipe packed with snow or ice | A blockage that can cause trouble at the next delivery | Clear it gently by hand; don't chip at it with a tool |
| Line crushed, kinked or pulled by falling ice | Damaged line, possible leak or fuel starvation | Stop the furnace if it's safe, call a service tech |
| Gauge cracked, stuck or missing | You can't trust the reading | Use the dipstick method and replace the gauge |
| Dent or scrape in the steel shell | Damage to the shell, severity depends on depth and rust | Have it looked at; a bad one means replacement |
| Shed or guard collapsed onto the tank | Physical protection has failed | Get the tank checked before the next delivery |

## If oil is leaking

Treat a leak as the whole job for the next hour. If it's safe to stop the flow, do it, keep people and pets away, and don't hose the spill or dig around it. Our guide to [what to do when an oil tank is leaking](/blog/oil-tank-leaking-what-to-do/) covers that first hour step by step, so use it.

There are legal steps as well. An owner who believes oil is leaking, or that water is getting into the system, has to inform the department immediately (s.18(1)), and then their heating oil delivery company (s.18(3)). After that the delivery company can't deliver until the system's integrity has been verified or it's been repaired or replaced to the administrator's satisfaction (s.18(4)).

The province runs a 24-hour environmental emergency line, and the department's [oil tank damage PDF](https://www.gov.nl.ca/eccc/files/env-protection-petroleum-pdf-oil-tank-damage.pdf) lays out what owners are expected to do. Your insurer should hear about it too. Whether a given policy covers a storm-damaged tank depends entirely on the wording, and [home insurance and oil tank leaks](/blog/does-insurance-cover-oil-tank-leaks/) explains what to ask.

## If the tank has shifted or tipped

Say your tank sits on the north side of a 1980s bungalow in Torbay, right under the roof edge. A February northeaster drops a slab of wet snow and ice from the eave, and in the morning the tank is leaning against the wall with one leg bent. Nothing's dripping, and the furnace is still running.

This is the point where most people want to be helpful. Don't. Don't refill it, don't try to jack it level and don't push it back onto the stand yourself.

Moving or repairing a tank isn't a casual fix. Under the regulations, to alter a system means to enlarge, reduce, upgrade, repair, replace, add or remove any part of it, or to change the configuration of the piping, filter, tank, physical protection or secondary containment (s.2(d)). An altered system has to be inspected by a licensed inspector (s.12(3)), and spill precautions apply while the work is going on (s.12(2)). So a nudge that feels harmless can turn the system into one that needs an inspection before it's used.

If you're on the shore road in [Torbay](/oil-tank-service-torbay/) or anywhere around the bay, the practical answer is the same: leave it, shut things down if you have to, and call someone who can do the job properly.

## Snow, ice and the vent after the storm

A plugged vent is the quiet one. It doesn't leak, it doesn't smell, and it doesn't show on the gauge. It shows up when the driver tries to fill the tank and the pressure has nowhere to go.

Clear snow and ice from the vent cap and the fill pipe by hand, gently, with a gloved hand or a soft brush. If it's frozen solid, don't beat at it with a shovel handle or a hammer. A cracked vent cap or a bent pipe is part of the system, and replacing parts of the system counts as an alteration.

Roof sheds are a particular Avalon hazard. Snow and ice that slides off a roof can bury a fill pipe in an hour, and a driver who can't find or reach it may leave without filling. If your delivery date is coming up, clear the path to the tank as well, not just the vent.

## When storm damage means a new tank

Some damage is a repair. A bent gauge, a blown vent cap, a crushed line can usually be fixed or swapped without touching the tank. Other damage is the tank itself: a dented shell, a seam that's weeping, a tank that's been on the ground in a snowbank for an hour with oil in it.

The age of the tank changes the maths. If a storm leaves you with a tank that's already close to the end of its tag, spending money on legs and patches rarely makes sense. A tank that's been leaking has to come out within a strict window anyway, and after a leak or water getting in the regulations compress the timeline to 10 days for emptying and dismantling, with 30 more for remediation (s.18(5)).

A 200-gallon painted steel tank, about 900 litres, is $3,200 installed, and a fibreglass tank is $3,850. Both prices include removing the old tank, and the registration by the installer's licensed inspector is normally part of that price. ## Frequently asked questions

### Can I push my oil tank back onto its stand after a storm?
No. Moving a tank counts as altering the system, and an altered system has to be inspected by a licensed inspector. A tank that's already leaning may also have a damaged line or a cracked fitting you can't see. Leave it alone and don't refill it until someone qualified has looked.

### Does the delivery driver have to refuse if the tank looks damaged?
The regulations require a driver to report deficiencies to the owner and the administrator, and heating oil can't be delivered to a system without a valid registration label. In practice, if the tag is expired or the tank looks unsafe, the truck leaves without filling. A shifted tank or a buried vent can lead to exactly that.

### How soon after a storm should I check the tank?
As soon as it's safe to walk out to it, and again after the next big wind. Daylight is better than a torch. If you find a leak, treat it as an emergency; if it's just a plugged vent or a bent gauge, it's still worth sorting before the next delivery.

### Will my insurance pay for a storm-damaged oil tank?
It depends on the policy wording, and nobody can promise you a yes from the outside. Call your insurer as soon as you find damage, take photos before you change anything and ask what's covered. The oil tank insurance guide on this site explains the questions to bring to that call.
