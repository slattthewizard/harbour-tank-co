# Oil Tank Legs, Stands and Pads: Why the Footing Matters as Much as the Tank

You're out with a shovel after a northeaster, clearing snow off the fill pipe, and the tank doesn't look right. One end sits a little lower than it did in October. The oil tank stand is the part nobody thinks about until it tilts, and by then the tank is leaning on its own piping.

A 200-gallon steel tank holds about 900 litres of oil. Full, that's a lot of weight pressing through four small feet onto whatever's underneath. The footing isn't a detail. It's half the installation.

## Why the oil tank stand matters as much as the tank

A tank is built to sit level. The oil needs to run to the outlet, the gauge only reads true on a level tank, and the outlet fitting is made for a tank that doesn't flex or twist. Let it lean and everything it's connected to takes the strain.

The line to the furnace is usually the first thing to suffer. Copper doesn't forgive a tank that shifts: it kinks, or it pulls at the fitting, and a weeping fitting is a leak, even a small one. The filter and the vent pipe are in the same boat.

That's worth remembering because the regulations treat the whole thing as one unit. The "system" is the tank plus the vent, fill and withdrawal piping and filters, right up to the furnace (s.2(n)). A stand that's moving puts the whole system at risk, not only the steel box on top.

There's also a legal angle. Section 15(6) says a system must have physical protection for the tank and its valves, gauges and piping or tubing. A sound stand on a solid footing is part of how a system meets that. The regulation doesn't give a recipe for how many blocks go under each leg, so we won't invent one. That's for the installer working to the manufacturer's instructions and the inspector signing off.

## What frost heave and soft ground do to a footing

Avalon ground moves. Water gets under a pad, freezes, and pushes up. A few weeks later it thaws and leaves a gap. Run that cycle enough times in a winter and one corner ends up lower than the rest.

The ground right beside a foundation is the worst spot. It was backfilled when the house went up, and backfill settles for years. A tank set on a couple of patio slabs there is sitting on the least stable soil on the lot.

Roof sheds make it worse. A roof that drops snow and meltwater on the tank puts load and moisture exactly where you don't want it, and a drip line can wash the gravel out from under a pad. Wind adds a push to a tank that's already out of true.

Picture a 1980s bungalow in Paradise with the tank on four legs, set on patio slabs beside the foundation. The slab nearest the wall has dropped over the years, so the tank leans toward the house by a couple of degrees. The first sign isn't the lean at all. It's a gauge that reads wrong, because the float no longer sits where it should.

## What to check on your legs, pad and lean

You don't need tools for this. A torch, your phone camera and five minutes will do it. Look from the side, not from above, because a lean is far easier to spot at eye level, and take a photo each autumn so you've got something to compare against in February.

| What you see | Risk | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| One leg sunk into the ground, tank leaning | Strain on the outlet fitting and line, wrong gauge reading, tipping in wind | Rebuild the footing and re-level; usually means draining the tank first, so quoted case by case |
| Rust at the foot of a leg | Leg weakens and can buckle; often a sign of constant damp underneath | Leg or stand replacement is possible; the tank's age and tag decide if it's worth doing |
| Cracked or broken pad | Uneven support, and frost gets into the cracks | Replace the pad, ideally with the tank off it |
| Water pooling under the tank | Damp on the tank's underside, rust from the bottom up | Fix the drainage or raise the base; sometimes a different spot on the lot is the real answer |
| Snow or ice piled against the legs | Hides problems, adds load, traps moisture on steel | Clear by hand and keep it clear; nothing needs a tool |
| Line pulled tight or kinked between tank and wall | Leak at the fitting, or a line that cracks on the next shift | Don't straighten it yourself; get the line and tank looked at |
| Lean plus a wet spot or oil smell | Possible leak | Stop, keep people and pets away, and follow the leak steps in the guide linked below |

Rust on a leg is a different question from rust on the tank shell. If you're trying to work out how bad a patch of rust is, our [guide to rust on an oil tank](/blog/rust-on-oil-tank/) covers painting, patching and replacing, and we won't repeat it here.

## When the legs can be fixed and when the tag decides it

Sometimes the legs really are the whole problem. A footing that's sunk can be rebuilt, a rusted leg can be replaced, and a tank with plenty of life left is worth keeping level.

Those jobs have a catch. Under the regulations, to "alter" a system means to repair, replace, add or remove any part of it, or change the physical protection (s.2(d)). An altered system has to be inspected by a licensed inspector (s.12(3)), spill precautions apply while the work happens (s.12(2)), and the owner registers the altered system (s.5(1)). Whether your particular fix counts is a conversation to have with the installer before anything gets dug up.

Fixing the stand doesn't reset the clock. The tag date is set by the tank's age and its row in the s.15 table, and none of that moves because the footing is new. If you want to see how those rows work, [how long oil tanks last](/blog/how-long-do-oil-tanks-last/) lays them out.

Say your tag is stamped 3-2027 and one leg has sunk. Fixing it means draining the tank, re-levelling, and paying for an inspection on a tank that's a few months from its last legal delivery. We'd put that money toward the replacement and build the footing right, once. A tank that's mid-life with a sunk corner is a different story, and fixing the footing is a fair call.

Legs and pads are quoted case by case, because the work depends on the ground, the access and the tank. A replacement is easier to price: $3,200 for a 200-gallon painted steel tank installed, or $3,850 for fibreglass, both including removal of the old tank. ## What a proper footing looks like on a new tank

On a swap, the stand and pad aren't an afterthought. A good installation starts with the ground. The base should be firm, level and free to drain, so water runs off rather than sitting under the tank, and the tank should be clear of the roof's drip line and out of the path of falling snow and ice.

A system has to meet CSA B139, the tank maker's instructions and the province's installation manual (s.5(2), s.11(1)). Different tanks carry different support instructions, which is another reason a new tank and its base are usually done together. We're not going to quote distances or dimensions here, because they belong to the code and the manufacturer, and the inspector checks them.

## Keeping it right between deliveries

Most stand problems announce themselves slowly. A look at the legs each time the truck comes is enough to catch them early.

Check the base is still level by eye. Keep snow and ice off the legs and away from the vent and fill pipe. Watch what the roof does above the tank after a thaw, because a new drip pattern can wash out a pad in a single season. The [pre-winter oil tank checklist](/blog/oil-tank-winter-checklist/) mentions legs briefly as part of a longer walk-around.

It's also worth knowing how the other end sees it. A delivery driver has to report deficiencies to the owner and the department immediately (s.13(2)). In practice, a tank that's visibly leaning can mean the truck leaves without filling it, and [why oil delivery gets refused](/blog/oil-delivery-refused/) explains how that plays out.

If a storm has already moved your tank, the department's [oil tank damage PDF](https://www.gov.nl.ca/eccc/files/env-protection-petroleum-pdf-oil-tank-damage.pdf) sets out what owners have to do. Don't push a shifted tank back yourself. Moving it is an alteration, and a tank full of oil is heavier than it looks.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I level my oil tank myself with shims or blocks?
We wouldn't. A tank with oil in it is heavy, the line and fittings are rigid, and lifting one corner can crack a fitting or kink the line. Moving or repairing the support is also an alteration under the regulations, so it needs a licensed inspector. Call someone in before you put a jack under it.

### Do I need an inspector just to replace the stand?
Under the regulations, replacing or repairing a part of the system counts as an alteration (s.2(d)), and an altered system must be inspected by a licensed inspector (s.12(3)). Whether a particular repair to the legs or the pad falls inside that is something to confirm with the installer before the work starts. When it does, the installer's licensed inspector handles the inspection and registration.

### Does a tank have to be perfectly level?
It has to sit the way the manufacturer's instructions say, and the system has to meet CSA B139. Some lean is more tolerable than others, and which is which depends on the tank and where its outlet is. If the gauge reads oddly or the line looks strained, the lean is already too much.

### Can a tank sit on paving stones or patio slabs?
Some do, and plenty sink. Slabs on backfilled ground near a foundation are the usual offenders. What your tank needs is a base that suits the tank, the ground and the manufacturer's instructions, and that's a call for the installer and the inspector rather than a rule of thumb.
