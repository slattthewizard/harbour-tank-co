## Why choosing an oil tank installer comes down to paperwork

Swapping a tank is a few hours of work with a truck, a pump and a crew. Plenty of people can do the lifting. What the law cares about is that the new system gets inspected, tagged and registered.

Under the regulations, the owner of a new system has to register it (s.5(1)), and nobody may fill a system that isn't registered. No registration means no delivery. You can end up with a shiny new tank that no truck will touch.

So the real job of a good installer is the part you don't see. They line up the licensed inspector, they handle the old tank properly, and they hand you a document at the end. Our [oil tank replacement](/oil-tank-replacement-st-johns/) work is built around that order of events, and we say "licensed and insured in Newfoundland and Labrador" because you should expect any installer to back up the same line with paper.

## The inspector question comes first

Only the inspection for registration needs a licence (s.20(1)). Installers work with a licensed inspector who inspects the new system, tags it and registers it. So your first question is simple: who is the inspector, and when do they come?

A good answer is boring. The installer has a licensed inspector they work with, the inspection happens on install day or close to it, and you get a copy of the certificate of registration. That certificate matters, because the province's own wording says insurers often ask for it when they insure a home, and lawyers often ask for it when a home is bought or sold.

Here's the answer that should worry you. "We'll register it later." Picture a split-level in Paradise with a tag stamped 3-2027. The new tank goes in on a Friday, the registration drifts into next month, and the first cold snap arrives before anyone has shown up. The truck driver reads the label, finds nothing valid, and leaves without filling (s.13(1)(b)(ii)).

One more point in your favour. A licensed inspector must recommend only necessary repairs or replacement (s.20(4)(b)). That's a rule on the inspector, and it's a fair thing to know if somebody is pushing extras.

Want to check a company's claims? The province publishes a list of companies with licensed inspectors through Service NL, and the department's [petroleum storage page](https://www.gov.nl.ca/eccc/env-protection/petroleum/) explains the registration program. We aren't going to name anyone on this page. Look the list up yourself and compare it to what you've been told.

## Questions to ask before you sign

Print this table or screenshot it on your phone. Any installer worth hiring will answer every row without squirming, and they'll answer in writing if you ask.

| Question to ask | A good answer | A red flag |
|---|---|---|
| Who inspects and registers the new system? | A licensed inspector the installer works with, booked for install day or close to it | "We'll register it later," or no mention of an inspector at all |
| Will I get the certificate of registration? | Yes, once the system is registered, and you keep a copy | A shrug, or "you won't need that" |
| Is the quote written and itemised? | Tank, installation, old tank removal, inspection and registration, each stated | One number sent in a text message |
| Is removal and disposal of the old tank included? | Yes, emptied, sludge removed, dismantled and disposed of, line removed or capped | The old tank stays "for now" behind the shed |
| What standard is the work done to? | CSA B139 and the manufacturer's instructions, named without hesitation | "We've always done it this way" |
| Are you insured? | Yes, and they'll show proof if you ask | A change of subject |
| What if the inspector finds a deficiency? | The installer says in writing who fixes it and who pays | Blame the inspector, or ask you to pay extra on the spot |
| How do I pay? | Invoice, receipt, terms stated up front | Cash only, no receipt, no paper |

The CSA row deserves a word. The regulations adopt CSA B139, and a system has to meet it along with the tank manufacturer's instructions and the province's installation manual (s.5(2), s.11(1)). You don't need to know what's in B139. You need an installer who does.

## Getting the old tank out properly

Removal is where corners get cut, because the old tank is heavy, dirty and nobody's favourite part of the job. The rule is clear, though. Within 30 days of disconnecting a tank, the owner has to empty it, remove the sludge and leftover oil, dismantle and dispose of it, remove or cap the piping, and restore the site (s.16(3)).

Notice who the rule is written for: you, the owner. If an installer drives off with half a job done, the 30-day clock is still yours. So ask exactly what "removal" means in the quote, and whether the line gets taken out or capped.

## Red flags that should end the conversation

You're not being fussy by walking away from any of these. We wouldn't put our name to a job that starts like this.

- No written quote, or a quote that's a single total with no lines.
- Cash only, with no receipt and no invoice.
- "We'll register it later," or any answer that skips the inspector.
- A tank delivered and set before anyone has looked at your tag, your stand or your line.
- Pressure to decide today because "the price goes up tomorrow."
- No answer, or a cranky answer, when you ask about removal and disposal of the old tank.

A tag date can create real urgency, and we won't pretend otherwise. If the expiry is close, the deadline is the tag, not the salesperson. If the truck has already refused you, our guide to [refused oil deliveries](/blog/oil-delivery-refused/) walks through what happens next.

## What happens if the inspector finds a problem

Say your tank goes in and the inspector spots a loose fitting, a line that isn't protected properly or a stand that isn't level. That isn't a disaster. It's the inspector doing the job, and the deficiency gets corrected before the system is registered.

What you want to know in advance is who does the correcting and who pays for it. If the problem is the installer's own work, the answer should be written down before the first bolt turns. If the problem was already sitting in your house, say an old line that has to be replaced, the answer is different, and an honest installer tells you that at the quote stage.

Physical protection for the tank, its valves, gauges and piping is a requirement too (s.15(6)). It's one of the things a good installer thinks about before the inspector arrives, not after.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I have to find the licensed inspector myself?
Normally no. Installers work with a licensed inspector who inspects, tags and registers the new system, so the question to put to the installer is who that inspector is and when they'll come. You can read how the registration program works on the department's petroleum page.

### Is the cheapest oil tank quote safe to take?
Not automatically. A low number is only a good deal if it covers the same job: the inspector, the registration, the removal of the old tank and a written itemised quote. If one quote leaves any of those out, you're comparing different jobs, and the gap usually turns up later as an extra charge or a delivery that gets refused.

### Can the installer register the tank for me after the swap?
Registration of a new system is the owner's responsibility under the regulations, and a system installed after the rules came in can't be used or filled until it's registered. In practice the installer's licensed inspector inspects the system and registers it as part of the job. If anyone suggests it can wait, treat that as a red flag and ask for a date in writing.

### What if my tag has already expired and the truck won't fill?
