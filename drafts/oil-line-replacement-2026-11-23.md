# Oil Line Replacement: When the Copper Line Has to Go Too

You walk out to the tank on a February morning and there's a dark stain on the gravel under the fitting. The tank is fine, the gauge reads half, and the oil is coming from the little copper line that runs to the house. That's the moment most people learn that oil line replacement is its own job, separate from the tank.

The line gets forgotten because it's thin and out of the way. It's also the part that carries oil into your house. This guide covers what counts as the line, the signs it's done, why the work usually happens during a tank swap, and what the rules say about it. If you'd rather skip the reading, send a photo of the line through the [contact form](/#contact) and ask for a free written estimate.

## What counts as the oil line

Under NLR 60/03, your oil "system" is more than the tank. Section 2(n) defines it as the tank plus all the vent, fill and withdrawal piping and filters, up to where the line connects to the furnace. So the copper tubing from the tank outlet to the burner, the fittings along the way and the filter on the run are all part of the system.

That matters for two reasons. The tag on your tank covers the system, not only the steel. And anything you change on the line is a change to a registered system, which brings the inspection rules into play.

Most Avalon homes have a single line, often copper, leaving the tank near the bottom or end and running along the foundation or through the wall to the furnace. Some are tucked in conduit, some are bare and some have been boxed in by a deck or a shed. If you aren't sure where yours goes, follow it from the tank to the wall and take a photo.

If your problem is a furnace that won't pull oil and you don't yet know why, start with the [furnace not getting oil checks](/blog/furnace-not-getting-oil/). The line is one of seven things to rule out. This article is for when the line itself is the suspect.

## Signs you're due for oil line replacement

Lines don't usually fail all at once. They weep, they get knocked, or they get squeezed. Here are the signs, side by side.

| Sign | Likely cause | Action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Damp or stained fitting at the tank or filter | Worn fitting, loose connection or a weeping joint | Have the fitting looked at; a repeat weep means replacing the section |
| Oil smell along the run, strongest near the wall | Pinhole or a failing joint in the line | Treat it as a leak until proven otherwise; see the smell guide |
| Visible kink or flattened section | Bumped, stepped on or bent during a past job | Replace the damaged section, don't straighten it |
| Line crushed or pulled | Ice off the roof, a fallen shovel, a shifted tank or a heavy snow load | Replace it, and check the tank hasn't moved |
| Line runs through concrete or under a slab | Original installation; you can't see or check it | Plan a re-route at the next swap |
| Furnace sputters or locks out with oil in the tank | Air getting in at a bad joint or a split line | Get a technician to trace it before replacing anything |

None of these is a reason to panic, but a few are a reason not to wait. A line that's actively dripping is a spill in progress.

## Oil line replacement is usually a tank-swap job

Picture a 1980s bungalow in Conception Bay South. The tank at the back wall is a 14 gauge steel tank with a tag stamped 5-2027, and the line from it runs along the foundation to a hole in the wall that's been painted over twice. The tank has to go before that tag runs out, and the line is the same age as the tank.

That's the usual story. The line was installed with the tank, it has the same decades behind it and it's easy to reach right now because the old tank is on its way out. Swapping the tank and leaving a tired line behind is like buying new tyres and keeping the old brake hoses.

So during a [tank replacement](/oil-tank-replacement-st-johns/), the crew will normally look at the line and tell you whether the old run can be reused. Sometimes it can, and sometimes a new section is the better call, particularly where there are kinks, damaged protection or a run through concrete.

## The rules: alteration, inspection and protection

Replacing a line isn't a casual change as far as the regulations go. Section 2(d) says to "alter" a system includes replacing or removing any part of it, or changing the configuration of the piping. A new line is an alteration.

An altered system gets inspected. Under s.12(3), a licensed inspector must inspect it, and under s.12(2) reasonable precautions have to be taken to prevent and contain spills while the work is under way. In practice, the installer's licensed inspector looks at the work and the altered system gets registered. You don't hire the inspector separately for a swap.

There's also physical protection. Section 15(6) says a system must have physical protection for the tank and its valves, gauges and piping or tubing. That's the reason a line along a driveway edge or a walkway sometimes gets a guard or a re-route. It's also why a line that's lying where snow slides off the roof is a candidate for moving.

The work has to meet the national installation code the regulations adopt, plus the tank maker's instructions. We won't quote clearances here, because they're the installer's and the inspector's call, and they're not something to guess from a web page. The full regulation text is on the [Assembly's site](https://www.assembly.nl.ca/legislation/sr/regulations/rc030060.htm) if you'd like to read it for yourself.

## When the tank goes, the line goes or gets capped

The same regulations cover what happens to an old line when a tank is taken out of service. Section 16(3) says that within 30 days of disconnecting a tank, the owner must empty it, remove the sludge and residual oil, dismantle and dispose of it, remove or cap the piping, and restore the site.

So an old line can't just be left connected to nothing. It's either removed or capped. If you're taking out a tank without replacing it, for a switch to a heat pump system or some other reason, the line is part of the same job. Our [oil tank removal service](/oil-tank-removal-st-johns/) covers the 30-day rule and the outdoor removal price.

Say you're on a lot in Torbay and the old tank sits on the north side, but the new one is going on the south side beside the furnace room. The old run is now a long, pointless piece of copper that could be a leak waiting to happen. The sensible answer is to remove it or cap it, and fit a short new run to the new position. Moving the line changes the configuration, so it's an alteration, and the inspection comes with it.

Some lines are easy. Others take thought.

A line buried in concrete is the hardest to live with. You can't see it, you can't check it and if it fails, the repair means breaking the slab. If your house has one, a swap is the best chance you'll get to re-route it above ground in a protected run. It's more work, and the price is quoted case by case.

A line boxed in behind a finished wall or under a deck is similar. Take a photo of whatever you can see and send it. Part of the estimate depends on access.

A line can also rub through where it passes the wall on older homes. Frost movement can work the line against the edge of the hole over the years until a pinhole opens. If you smell oil in the basement near that wall and the tank gauge looks normal, our [oil smell guide](/blog/oil-smell-in-basement/) walks through what to check first.

If you find liquid oil on the ground, on the floor or dripping off the line, stop the flow if it's safe to, keep people and pets away, don't hose or dig, and phone the province's 24-hour environmental emergency line and your insurer. The first-hour steps are in the [leaking tank guide](/blog/oil-tank-leaking-what-to-do/). The department's [petroleum storage page](https://www.gov.nl.ca/eccc/env-protection/petroleum/) explains an owner's duties.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I replace the oil line myself?
Replacing any part of the system, including the piping, is an alteration under the regulations, and a licensed inspector has to inspect an altered system. That's a job for an installer who works with a licensed inspector. A homemade fix on a line that carries fuel oil isn't one we'd put our name to.

### Does a new tank come with a new oil line?
Not automatically. The crew looks at the existing run and tells you whether it can be reused. If it's kinked, damaged, leaking or buried where nobody can check it, a new line is the better call, and the quote should say so.

### How do I know if the line is leaking or the tank is?
Look at where the oil is. A wet fitting or a stain along the run points to the line, while a wet base or a dark patch under the tank points to the shell. Either way, treat it as a leak, keep people away and read the first-hour guide before you do anything else.

### Is a leaking oil line something I have to report?
Yes. Under s.18(1), an owner who believes oil is leaking must immediately inform the department, and under s.18(3) the heating oil delivery company too. The delivery company can't deliver again until the system has been verified or repaired to the administrator's satisfaction.
