# Double Wall Oil Tank Explained: Roth, Double-Bottom and What the Terms Mean

## What a double wall oil tank is, in plain terms

A standard outdoor steel tank is a single shell. One layer of painted steel holds your oil, and if that layer lets go, the oil is on the ground.

A double wall oil tank adds a second layer. There's an inner tank that holds the oil and an outer wall around it, with a gap between the two. If the inner shell weeps, the oil lands in the gap instead of the yard or the basement floor. That gap has a proper name in the regulations: the interstice.

The idea is simple. You don't stop a tank from ever failing, because nothing does that. You make the failure small, slow and easy to spot.

Some of these tanks have a way to monitor the gap, so a problem shows up on a gauge or alarm before anyone smells oil. What that monitoring looks like depends on the tank and the installer, so ask to see it on the actual model you're being quoted, not on a brochure.

## Roth, double containment and the other names on your quote

Quotes use different words for the same family of tanks, and that's where the confusion starts. The terms usually line up like this.

"Double wall" and "double containment" are everyday ways of saying a tank with a second layer. "Secondary containment" is the regulation's phrase for it. "Roth" is a brand, and it's the double-wall tank named on our price list: a Roth double-containment tank installed inside is $3,850.

The word that trips people up is "double bottom". That's a design some steel tanks use, where there's an extra layer under the floor of the tank, since the floor is where water collects and rust starts. It isn't automatically the same thing as a full double wall tank. We'll come back to that.

## How the legal life works for a double wall oil tank

Single-wall steel gets a fixed life out of a table. Gauge, outlet position and whether the tank is inside or outside decide it, and the numbers run from 10 to 25 years, counted from the effective date on the registration. The [life table guide](/blog/how-long-do-oil-tanks-last/) walks through every row.

Double-wall tanks don't use that table. Under s.15(2) to (5) of NLR 60/03, tanks that aren't mild steel, and tanks with secondary containment, get a life set by the administrator, capped at 50 years. For a tank with a monitored interstice, the wording covers tanks sited outside with precipitation kept out of the gap, or tanks sited inside.

Two things follow from that. First, the tag decides everything, not the brochure. The number stamped on it is the date your oil supplier's driver reads at the fill pipe. Second, the cap is a ceiling, not a promise. The administrator sets the life for the tank in front of them.

Fibreglass tags have been seen stamped all the way to 2061, which gives you an idea of how different this is from a single-wall steel tank with a 15-year clock. If you want a refresher on reading the stamp, the [HOST tag guide](/blog/how-to-read-host-tag/) is short.

## Double-bottom steel: ask before you assume

Picture a bungalow in Torbay where the quote lists a "double bottom" steel tank at a price that sits close to plain steel. It's tempting to read that as a double wall tank at a bargain. It might be nothing of the sort.

Here's the question to ask: how will this tank be classed and registered, and what expiry will the tag carry? If the answer is "the same as any steel tank, by gauge and outlet", then you've bought a steel tank with a better floor, which isn't a bad thing, but it's a 10 to 25 year tank, not a 50 year one.

If the answer is that the tank has secondary containment with a monitored interstice, you're in the s.15(2) group, and the licensed inspector will say what life goes on the tag. Either way, get it in writing before the swap, because the expiry is stamped once and lives on the tank.

The same advice holds for any unfamiliar word on a quote. Our [guide to what a quote should include](/blog/oil-tank-quote-whats-included/) is a good checklist for pressing on the details.

## Inside or outside: where a double wall tank earns its place

A double wall tank makes the most sense in places where a leak is expensive.

Inside a basement, that's obvious. A tank in a finished basement or next to a laundry room is a long way from a hose and a bucket, and a leak into a concrete floor is slow and smelly to clean. A containment layer takes a lot of that worry off the table, which is why the Roth on our price list is the indoor option, at $3,850.

Outside, the argument changes. Salt air, freeze-thaw and wind-driven rain are what shorten the life of outdoor steel along the Avalon coast, and an outer wall helps with a leak but doesn't stop the steel shell from ageing. A fibreglass tank takes the rust out of the picture altogether and costs the same $3,850, which is $650 more than steel. If your tank sits on a Conception Bay South shoreline lot, that comparison is worth a proper look.

Say your tank sits under the drip line of a roof in a Mount Pearl subdivision, where the snow slides off in the first thaw and buries the fill pipe. A tougher tank doesn't fix the siting. Move the problem or protect the tank, and talk to the installer about both.

Whichever type you pick, moving a tank, changing its configuration or swapping it is an alteration under s.2(d), so a licensed inspector inspects it and the new system gets registered. That's part of how the work is done, and the installer's licensed inspector tags and registers the new system as part of a normal install. ## Frequently asked questions

### Is a Roth tank the same as a double wall oil tank?
Roth is a brand, and the Roth tank on our price list is a double-containment tank, which is a type of double wall tank. Not every double wall tank is a Roth. Ask the installer which model is being quoted and what the tag will say.

### Does a double wall oil tank last 50 years?
The regulations cap the legal life of these tanks at 50 years, and the administrator sets the actual figure. The cap is a ceiling. The expiry stamped on your tag is the number that counts when a driver reads it.

### Is a double-bottom steel tank the same as double wall?
Not automatically. Double bottom describes an extra layer under the floor of some steel tanks, while double wall means a second layer around the whole tank. Ask the installer how the tank will be classed and registered, and get that in writing.

### Do I need a licensed inspector for a new double wall tank?
Yes. Inspection for registration requires a licensed inspector under s.20(1), and the installer's licensed inspector tags and registers the new system. That's normally part of the installed price, and it's what produces the confirmation of registration that lawyers and insurers often ask for.
