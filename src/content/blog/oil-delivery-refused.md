## Why oil delivery gets refused in Newfoundland

Every registered heating oil tank in the province carries a small metal HOST tag. HOST stands for Heating Oil Storage Tank, and the tag has a six-digit number and an expiry date stamped as month and year. A tag that reads 7-2028 means the tank can't be used after 31 July 2028. If you've never looked closely at yours, the [HOST tag post](/blog/how-to-read-host-tag/) shows where it sits and how to read it.

The rules come from the [Heating Oil Storage Tank System Regulations, 2003](https://www.assembly.nl.ca/legislation/sr/regulations/rc030060.htm), known as NLR 60/03. Section 13 is the one that matters at delivery time. It says heating oil can't be delivered to a system that doesn't have a valid, unexpired registration label showing the registration is current. That's been the law since 1 August 2009.

There's a second reason a fill gets refused, and it catches people whose tag still has years on it. The same section says the driver can't deliver to a system that doesn't comply with the regulations. A stand that's rusted through, a tank leaning on its pad, a wet seam or a fill pipe that's come loose all count. The driver is looking at the whole system, not just the date on the tag.

So there are really three versions of a refused delivery:

- **The tag has expired.** The most common one, and usually a surprise, because the tank looks fine.
- **The tag is current but the tank isn't.** Rust, a bad stand, a leak or a damaged line.
- **The tag is missing, damaged or doesn't belong to that tank.** Rarer, but it happens after a DIY swap or a house sale.

Whichever one you've got, the way back to heat is the same: a system that passes, a current tag, and a fresh delivery booked.

## What the driver has to do after a refused oil delivery

This is the part most homeowners don't know, and it explains the slip on the door. Under section 13(2) of NLR 60/03, a driver who finds a deficiency has to report it immediately, both to you and to the province. It's a duty written into the regulation, not a courtesy.

In practice that means the driver writes down the tag number, the expiry date and what they saw, leaves a copy with you or on the door, and the details go in to the province. If nobody was home, you may only find out when you notice the gauge hasn't moved.

Two things you shouldn't do while you sort this out:

- **Don't top up the tank from jerry cans.** Section 4(1) of the regulations prohibits operating a system that's past its allowed service life. The problem is the tank, not the oil level.
- **Don't try to patch, weld or tighten a leaking tank yourself.** If the refusal was for a wet seam or a weeping fitting, leave it alone, put a tray or cat litter under it, and get a professional to look. A steel tank that's rusting from the inside can open up wider when you put a wrench on it.

## Getting a new tank, a new tag and the truck back in the driveway

For the common case, an expired tag on a steel tank, the fix is a new tank. The expiry date on the tag is the end of the service life the regulations allow for that tank, and there's no re-inspection that adds years to a mild-steel tank once it's reached that date. Section 15 sets those limits: an outdoor single-wall 14-gauge tank with an end or top outlet gets 10 years, the same tank in 12-gauge gets 15, and the longest any steel tank gets is 25 years indoors with a bottom outlet.

If the refusal was for a deficiency on a tank whose tag still has time on it, the answer depends on what the driver saw. A rusted stand or a loose fill pipe can sometimes be fixed and the system checked again by a licensed inspector. A wet seam or rust through the shell means the tank is done, whatever the tag says. And if the tag is within a couple of years of expiry anyway, paying to repair a stand under a tank you'll be replacing soon rarely makes sense.

Here's what the replacement itself looks like, start to finish:

1. **Site visit and written estimate.** Free, anywhere on the Avalon. You get a number in writing before anyone touches the tank.
2. **Pump-out.** Whatever oil is left gets pumped out. Ask what happens to it before you agree to a quote, so you're not paying for the same fuel twice.
3. **Old tank out.** Disconnected, drained, hauled away and disposed of. For an outdoor tank that's included in the replacement price.
4. **New tank in.** On a sound stand and pad, with new lines if the old ones won't pass. Lines and stands show up as their own line on the estimate.
5. **Inspection and tag.** The installer's licensed inspector checks the system, tags it and registers it with the province. That's included with the install, not a separate bill.
6. **Rebook the delivery.** Call the fuel company with the new tag number and expiry date. From a refused delivery to oil in the new tank is usually within the week.

## How to make sure this doesn't happen again

A refused delivery is a one-time event if you do two things afterward.

First, write down the expiry date on your new tag somewhere you'll see it. The fridge, the side of the furnace, or a phone reminder set for the spring before it expires. A steel tank outdoors can be as short as 10 years.

Second, look at the tank every fall before the first delivery. Legs, stand, pad, seams, filter, fill and vent pipes, and the line into the house. If you see rust bubbling under the paint, a lean, or a dark patch on the ground, deal with it before the driver does.
