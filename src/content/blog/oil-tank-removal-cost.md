## Outdoor tank removal: why it's a flat $500

An outdoor tank is the easy one, and the price shows it. Most Avalon homes have a 200-gallon steel tank on a stand beside the house, and getting it out is a known quantity, which is why we can put a flat number on it.

For $500 the crew pumps out whatever oil is left, scrapes the sludge and water out of the bottom, disconnects the supply line and filter, and takes the tank off the stand. Fill and vent pipes get capped or pulled so no driver can ever fill a tank that isn't there. Then the stand, pad and tank leave on the truck, and the ground gets raked before we go.

That's the typical outdoor job. If yours is on a stand by the house with a clear path to the road, $500 is the number to expect.

## Indoor and basement oil tank removal cost: why it's quoted case by case

Basement tanks are a different animal. A full-size steel tank doesn't fit through a standard door, and it definitely doesn't go up a set of 1970s basement stairs in one piece. So the tank has to be drained, cleaned, cut down into sections right where it sits, and carried out a piece at a time.

How long that takes depends on things we can't know from the driveway: how many stairs and whether they turn, how wide the doorway is, whether the tank is tucked behind the furnace, and how much sludge has settled in the bottom over the years. Every one of those changes the hours, and the hours change the price.

That's why you won't see a flat rate for indoor removal from us or from any honest contractor on the Avalon. What you will get is a free site visit and a written quote before any work starts, and the number won't grow on the invoice unless we find something and you sign off on it first.

One thing worth knowing: cutting into a steel tank that held heating oil is not a weekend project. There's fume risk, there's residual oil in every seam, and there's the sludge, which has to go to a waste-oil handler and not down the floor drain. If your tank is in the basement, [get a written quote](/#contact) rather than a Sawzall.

## What changes the price up or down

Every removal starts from the same base: drain it, clean it, dismantle it, haul it. What moves the number is the house. These are the things we look at on a site visit.

- **Where the tank sits.** Outdoor on a stand by the house is the $500 baseline. Behind a fence, down a bank, or wedged between the house and the shed takes longer. In the basement, see above.
- **How much is left in it.** A tank with 20 gallons of clean oil is quick to pump out. A tank with a foot of [sludge](/blog/water-in-oil-tank/), water and rust flakes in the bottom takes real time to clean.
- **Whether it leaked.** A sound tank comes out clean. One that's weeping at a seam or has a soft bottom needs more care to move, and any oil in the ground is a separate job entirely. Our [oil spill cleanup cost](/blog/oil-tank-leaking-what-to-do/) post covers what that side looks like.
- **The lines and the stand.** Removal includes capping or pulling the fill and vent pipes. If a new tank is going in and the old copper supply line won't pass inspection, or the stand is rusted through, those get replaced too and show up as their own line on the estimate.
- **Fibreglass versus steel.** Steel gets cut down and goes for scrap. Fibreglass can't be scrapped, so it goes to a facility that accepts it.
- **Timing.** A planned removal on a Tuesday in October is straightforward. A leaking tank at 11 p.m. in February may carry an after-hours rate, and that varies by contractor. Ask up front; we tell you before the truck rolls.
- **Whether a new tank is going in.** This is the big one, and it gets its own section.

None of these are gotchas. They're the difference between an honest quote and a guess. If you want to know exactly what should be listed on yours, our [what's included in an oil tank quote](/blog/oil-tank-quote-whats-included/) post walks through it line by line.

## The 30-day rule, and why waiting costs more

The part of this that catches people out isn't the price. It's the clock. Under section 16(3) of [NLR 60/03, the Heating Oil Storage Tank System Regulations](https://www.assembly.nl.ca/legislation/sr/regulations/rc030060.htm), once a tank is disconnected the owner has 30 days to empty it, remove the sludge and residual oil, dismantle and dispose of it, remove or cap the piping, and restore the site. If the tank leaked or took on water, section 18(5) cuts that to 10 days, with another 30 days allowed for cleaning up the ground.

That's not a suggestion from us. It's the law, and it applies to every house from Mount Pearl to Carbonear. The province's [petroleum storage page](https://www.gov.nl.ca/eccc/env-protection/ics/petroleum/) is where the rules live if you want to read them yourself.

Why does waiting cost more? Three reasons.

- **Rust doesn't stop.** A tank that was sound in June can be weeping by November, which turns a $500 job into a $500 job plus a soil cleanup.
- **Closing day.** "I'll deal with it later" tends to become "the buyers' lawyer wants proof it's gone" the week before a sale. Insurers and realtors ask about tank registration all the time.
- **Winter.** Digging a tank out of a snowdrift in January takes longer than lifting it off a dry stand in September, and after-hours calls carry rates that daytime bookings don't.

A word on safety before you do anything yourself. Shutting the supply valve at the tank is fine if you can reach it easily. Anything past that, like disconnecting lines, moving a tank, or cutting one open, is a job for a crew with the right gear and a place to send the oil.

Residual heating oil and sludge can't go in the garbage or down a drain, and a tank that's been "drained" still has enough in it to make a mess of a lawn or a basement floor.
